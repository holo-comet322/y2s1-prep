Concept 1 of 5

Why this topic exists

Week 2 treated gates as perfect logical devices — a 1 is a 1, instantly. Real gates are physical circuits made of real transistors on real wires, so they have imperfections: exact voltages that drift depending on what's driving them, delays before an output actually changes, and limits on how many other gates one gate can safely feed. This week is about quantifying those imperfections so a designer can guarantee a circuit works "even under the worst conditions" — not just in the best case.

Everything here splits into two buckets: static (DC) behaviour — what happens when signals are sitting still, and dynamic (AC) behaviour — what happens the instant a signal changes.

Static behaviour: logic levels and noise margins

Four voltage thresholds define how "confident" a gate is when reading or producing a logic level:

VOHminthe minimum voltage a gate guarantees when its output is HIGH
VIHminthe minimum voltage the next gate needs to see to reliably read a HIGH
VILmaxthe maximum voltage the next gate still reliably reads as LOW
VOLmaxthe maximum voltage a gate guarantees when its output is LOW

For everything to work, an output's worst case must always be safely inside the next gate's input requirement: VOHmin must exceed VIHmin, and VOLmax must stay below VILmax. The spare room between them is called the DC noise margin — literally how much unwanted electrical noise the signal can tolerate before being misread.

Low-state noise margin = VILmax − VOLmax
High-state noise margin = VOHmin − VIHmin

What it calculates: the spare voltage room between a driving gate's guaranteed output and the next gate's minimum requirement, in each logic state. When to use it: once you have all four V-thresholds for the driving and receiving gate families — a bigger margin means more tolerance for noise before a bit gets misread.

Analogy Think of noise margin like the buffer space when parallel parking: VOLmax is where your bumper actually ends up, VILmax is the furthest line you're still allowed to cross without being "in the next bay". The gap between them is how much you could be nudged (by a bump, a gust of wind, electrical noise) and still be read correctly. A bigger noise margin means a more forgiving, more reliable design.

Practice — continues Week 2's CMOS-build method
Tutorial 3, Q1
Tests: building a CMOS circuit for a compound (multi-level) expression — carries over the AOI/OAI method from Week 2

Draw a minimum-transistor CMOS circuit for f(a,b,c,d) = (a + b·c)′·d + a′ (no need to simplify first).

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Approach

This expression is nested two levels deep, so it can't be built as one single AOI stage — decompose it into stages, and notice a shortcut: a single CMOS gate naturally produces an inverted output, so if you need a term that's already complemented, that's often free rather than extra work.

  1. Build Stage 1 as one CMOS OR-AND-INVERT-style gate whose NMOS pull-down network is "a in parallel with (b series c)" — its natural output is exactly p = (a+b·c)′, no extra inverter needed, since that's the exact term the final expression wants.
  2. You'll also need a′ (true complement of a) as a standalone signal — build a plain inverter for that.
  3. Build Stage 2 as a second CMOS gate combining p (AND with d) and a′ (OR'd in): its pull-down network should conduct on f′ = (p·d + a′)′ = a·(p + d′). That means: transistor-a in series with [transistor-p in parallel with transistor-d′] — which means you also need an inverter for d to get d′.

Answer (structure): one OAI-style gate for (a+bc)′, one inverter each for a and d, and one more AOI-style gate combining everything for the final output f. Total: 2 two-level CMOS gates + 2 inverters.

Practice — continues Week 2's CMOS-build method
Tutorial 3, Q2
Tests: the same decomposition skill, plus recognising a free NAND

Draw a CMOS circuit for f = [a + (c+d)·(b+c)]·(a′+c′).

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Approach

Spot the shortcut first: (a′+c′) is exactly what a plain 2-input NAND gate computes (NAND(a,c) = (a·c)′ = a′+c′ by De Morgan) — so that whole bracket is just one NAND gate, no extra inverters needed.

The other bracket, [a + (c+d)·(b+c)], is a 3-level nested OR-AND-OR — build (c+d) and (b+c) first (two small OR stages, or fold them into one bigger AOI-style network), AND those two results together, then OR with a, remembering the whole thing comes out inverted from a single CMOS stage — so if you build it as one big OAI network you'll get its complement for free, and would need one more inverter to get back the true (uninverted) form before the final AND with the NAND(a,c) term.

Answer (structure): one NAND(a,c) gate for the second bracket; one OAI-style network + inverter for the first bracket; a final AND gate (itself built as NAND + inverter) combining the two. This is a good one to actually sketch out on paper, transistor by transistor, using the Week 2 method — the value here is in the decomposition, not in memorising this specific answer.

The tutorial's "Additional Home Exercise" (drawing a CMOS circuit for Y = (A·B̄+C)·D̄·E + Ā·C̄) is good optional extra practice using the exact same decomposition method as Q1/Q2 above — identify the innermost bracket, realise it as one CMOS stage, add inverters only where a true (uncomplemented) signal is needed for the next stage.

Concept 2 of 5

Input/output currents, sinking vs sourcing

Gates don't just care about voltage — the current flowing in and out matters too, because every real output has some resistance, and pushing too much current through that resistance drags the voltage away from where it should be.

  • Sinking current: current flows from the supply, through the external load, and into the gate's output pin, down to ground (the gate's NMOS is ON, pulling to ground).
  • Sourcing current: current flows out of the gate's output pin, through the load, to ground (the gate's PMOS is ON, pulling to the supply).

IOLmax and IOHmax are the most current an output can sink/source while still keeping its voltage within spec; IILmax/IIHmax are how much current an input actually draws.

Fanout: how many gates can one gate feed?

Fanout (HIGH state) = IOHmax / IIHmax    Fanout (LOW state) = IOLmax / IILmax

What it calculates: the maximum number of next-stage inputs one output can safely drive, in each logic state. When to use it: once you know the driver's IOHmax/IOLmax and the load's IIHmax/IILmax — compute both states and keep the smaller result.

Compute both — a gate is only as good as its worse state, so the actual fanout is the smaller of the two, rounded down to a whole number (you can't safely drive "16.3 gates").

Analogy Fanout is exactly like asking "how many phone chargers can I plug into one power strip before the voltage sags too much for any of them to charge properly?" Each device (input) draws a bit of current; the strip (output) can only supply so much before it can't guarantee full voltage to everyone.

Overloading fanout doesn't cause a clean failure — it degrades voltage margins, slows down propagation delay, increases rise/fall time, and raises operating temperature, all at once, which is why "exceeding fanout" can cause maddening intermittent bugs rather than an obvious break.

Unused inputs must never be left floating: a high-impedance CMOS input picks up stray noise easily enough to randomly read as HIGH, causing unpredictable glitches. Always tie unused inputs to a defined level.

Practice — tests concepts 1 & 2
Tutorial 3, Q3
Tests: fanout and noise margin from a datasheet table

From the 74AS/74ALS columns of the family table, find: (a) 74AS driving 74AS fanout, (b) 74AS driving 74ALS fanout, (c) 74ALS driving 74AS fanout, (d) 74AS noise margins.

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Approach

Always pair the driver's output current spec with the load's input current spec, for both LOW and HIGH states, then take the smaller of the two resulting fanouts.

(a) 74AS→74AS: LOW: 20mA / 0.5mA = 40. HIGH: 2mA / 20µA = 100. Fanout = min(40,100) = 40.

(b) 74AS→74ALS: LOW: 20mA (74AS output) / 0.2mA (74ALS input) = 100. HIGH: 2mA / 20µA = 100. Fanout = 100.

(c) 74ALS→74AS: LOW: 4mA (74ALS output) / 0.5mA (74AS input) = 8. HIGH: 0.4mA / 20µA = 20. Fanout = min(8,20) = 8.

(d) 74AS noise margins: Low-state = VILmax−VOLmax = 0.8−0.5 = 0.3V. High-state = VOHmin−VIHmin = 2.7−2.0 = 0.7V.

Practice — tests concept 2
Tutorial 3, Q4
Tests: fanout with a shared/mixed load

A 74AC/ACT gate drives forty 74AS gates plus some number of 74ALS gates. What's the maximum number of 74ALS gates?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Approach

The driver has one fixed current budget (IOLmax, IOHmax) that must be shared across all the loads combined — add up everyone's current demand and keep it under the driver's limit, checking both LOW and HIGH states.

LOW state (binding constraint): driver IOLmax = 24mA. Forty 74AS gates draw 40 × 0.5mA = 20mA, leaving 4mA. Each 74ALS gate draws IILmax = 0.1mA, so 4mA / 0.1mA = 40 more gates.

HIGH state (not binding): driver IOHmax = 24mA = 24000µA. Forty 74AS gates draw 40×20µA=800µA, leaving 23200µA; at 20µA per 74ALS gate that's room for 1160 — far more than the LOW-state limit allows.

Answer: the LOW state is the bottleneck, so the maximum is 40 additional 74ALS gates.

Concept 3 of 5

Dynamic behaviour: nothing changes instantly

Rise time trtime for the output to climb from LOW to HIGH
Fall time tftime for the output to drop from HIGH to LOW
Propagation delay tptime between an input changing and the corresponding output change — this is the "reaction time" of the gate
tpHL / tpLHpropagation delay specifically for an output going HIGH→LOW, or LOW→HIGH (these are often slightly different from each other)
Analogy Propagation delay is like the time between a sprinter hearing the starting gun and their foot actually leaving the blocks — there's always some reaction time, even in a "perfect" system. Chain enough gates together (like a relay race) and those tiny delays add up to something you can actually measure and must design around.

Practice — tests concept 3
Tutorial 3, Q5
Tests: propagation delay — tracing it through a multi-gate circuit

Circuit: a → NOT → x; b,c → XOR → y; x,y → OR → z. Every gate has 2ns propagation delay. Given the waveforms for a, b, c, complete x, y, z.

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Approach

The golden rule: each output is a delayed, logically-combined copy of its inputs — shift the logical combination of the inputs to the right by exactly one gate-delay (2ns) to get that gate's output. Do it stage by stage; delays add up as the signal passes through more gates (x and y are each 2ns behind their inputs; z is 2ns behind x/y, i.e. 4ns behind the original a/b/c change that caused it).

Reading the given waveforms: a toggles every 5ns starting LOW (transitions at t=5,10,15,20,25,30,35); b toggles every 10ns starting LOW (transitions at t=10,20,30); c is LOW until t=20, then HIGH.

x = a′, shifted +2ns: transitions exactly 2ns after every a-transition → x changes at t=7,12,17,22,27,32,37 (starting HIGH, since a(0)=0).

y = (b⊕c), shifted +2ns: b⊕c is itself a single pulse, HIGH during [10,30) (LOW elsewhere) — shift the whole pulse 2ns later: y is LOW until t=12, HIGH during [12,32), LOW again from t=32.

z = (x OR y), shifted +2ns (i.e. 4ns after the original a/b/c change): combining the shifted x and y pulses gives z HIGH for [0,9), LOW for [9,14), HIGH for [14,39), dropping again right at the very end (t=39) of this window.

The exact numbers matter less than the method: redraw each input pulse shifted right by the accumulated delay, then OR/AND/XOR the shifted copies — never the original, un-delayed inputs.

Concept 4 of 5

Wired logic and driving other devices

An open-drain output can only actively pull LOW (never actively drive HIGH); a pull-up resistor supplies the HIGH level instead. Tying several open-drain outputs together onto one shared line creates a wired-AND: the shared line only reads HIGH if every connected output is "open" (inactive); any single output pulling LOW drags the whole line LOW — free AND logic with no extra gate.

Driving an LED: a plain resistor calculation — Ohm's law across whatever voltage is left after the supply, the LED's forward drop, and the gate's own output-low voltage: R = (VCC − VLED − VOLmax) / ILED.

Concept 5 of 5

Logic families: same logic, different electrical recipe

Parts like 74HC, 74HCT, 74AHC, 74LS, 74AS all implement the same logic functions (same "nn" part number) but with different speed/power/voltage tradeoffs. A few naming landmarks: HC = high-speed CMOS levels; HCT = high-speed CMOS but with TTL-compatible input thresholds (so it can be driven directly by older TTL chips); TTL families (74, 74S, 74LS, 74AS, 74ALS, 74F) are a completely different (bipolar, not MOS) technology with their own fixed 0–0.8V / 2.0–5.0V levels. Power dissipation for CMOS scales as P = CPD·VCC²·f — note the squared voltage term, which is exactly why the industry has been pushing supply voltages down (3.3V, 2.5V, 1.8V…) as chips get denser: halving voltage cuts dynamic power to a quarter.

✍️ Write This Down

Copy these into your own notes:

  • VOHmin, VIHmin, VILmax, VOLmax — and that noise margin = the gap between an output guarantee and the next input's requirement.
  • Low noise margin = VILmax − VOLmax; High noise margin = VOHmin − VIHmin.
  • Sinking current = current flows INTO the output (pulling to ground); sourcing = current flows OUT of the output (pulling to supply).
  • Fanout = min(IOLmax/IILmax, IOHmax/IIHmax), always rounded down to an integer.
  • When a driver feeds mixed loads, add up everyone's current demand in each state separately and compare against the driver's single budget — check both states, the smaller one wins.
  • Propagation delay tp = time between an input change and the resulting output change; tpHL and tpLH can differ.
  • To trace waveforms through a multi-gate circuit: compute the logical combination of the (undelayed) inputs first, then shift the whole result right by the accumulated gate delay.
  • Wired-AND: tying open-drain outputs together gives AND for free — any single LOW output pulls the shared line LOW.
  • CMOS dynamic power ∝ VCC², which is why supply voltages keep shrinking (3.3V → 2.5V → 1.8V…) as chips get denser.

🟥 Redstone Build: Feeling Propagation Delay

Minecraft redstone is one of the only "logic" systems you can play with that has a very real, very visible equivalent of propagation delay — a redstone repeater. This week's build is about making delay and fanout tangible rather than abstract.

Build 1 — see propagation delay directly

  1. Build the NOT-gate / AND-gate combinations from Week 2's redstone builds.
  2. Place a redstone repeater on the output wire of one of your gates, and set it to its slowest delay setting (right-click to cycle 1–4 ticks; each tick ≈ 0.1s, so 4 ticks ≈ 0.4s of visible delay — much slower than a real logic gate's nanoseconds, but the same *idea*).
  3. Flip the input lever and watch: there's now a clearly visible gap between "cause" (lever flip) and "effect" (lamp changing) — this is literally tp made large enough to watch with your own eyes instead of measuring with an oscilloscope.

Build 2 — reproduce Tutorial Q5's circuit and watch the glitch

  1. Build the exact 3-gate circuit from Tutorial Q5 (NOT on a → x; XOR on b,c → y; OR on x,y → z), using a repeater on each gate's output set to represent "2ns" consistently (pick, say, 1 tick per gate for all three).
  2. Wire up three lever/comparator-clock inputs for a, b, c so you can toggle them independently and watch z on a redstone lamp.
  3. Toggle inputs at different times and watch z: you'll see it settle a beat after the last relevant gate's repeater fires — a physical demonstration of why real circuits need "settling time" before you can trust an output, and why fast, uncoordinated input changes can cause brief, incorrect glitches on the output before everything catches up.

Optional — fanout as a physical limit

Vanilla redstone dust signal strength decays by 1 per block travelled (from 15 down to 0), which is a rough physical echo of "loading" a signal too far — you have to insert a repeater (which also re-powers the signal back to full strength) roughly every 15 blocks, similar to how noise margins force real designs to "refresh" a signal before it degrades past the point of being read correctly.
