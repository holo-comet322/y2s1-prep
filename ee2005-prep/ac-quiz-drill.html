📘 Speed & slip

Q1

Synchronous speed of an 8-pole, 60 Hz machine (rpm)?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 900 rpm

What it's asking. Find how fast the rotating magnetic field made by the stator winding spins, in revolutions per minute. This is the synchronous speed, the pace-setter speed that the rotor is always trying to follow.

Steps needed. (1) Read the pole count p and the supply frequency f from the question. (2) Put them into the synchronous-speed formula. (3) Read the answer as revolutions per minute of the field.

ns = 120 f / p
nssynchronous speed in rpm: how fast the stator's rotating magnetic field turns. It is also the speed the rotor would have if slip were zero.
fsupply frequency in Hz (cycles per second). Here 60 Hz.
pnumber of poles (north plus south, so a 4-pole machine has 2 pole pairs). It is a property of how the winding was built.
120a constant that bundles two conversions: 60 seconds per minute, and a factor 2 because one electrical cycle moves the field past one pole PAIR (2 poles).

What it calculates: the speed of the stator's rotating field, which is fixed entirely by the supply frequency and the pole count. Why we need it: every slip, rotor-frequency and torque calculation starts from ns. When it is valid: any AC machine with a rotating field on a sinusoidal supply; p must be poles, not pole pairs.

Step 1 — read the data. p = 8 and f = 60 Hz.

Step 2 — calculate. Substitute and divide.

ns = 120 × 60 / 8 = 7200 / 8 = 900 rpm

Where the 120 comes from. 8 poles is 4 pole pairs. Each electrical cycle sweeps the field past one pole pair, so the field needs 4 cycles for one full turn. At 60 cycles per second that is 60 / 4 = 15 revolutions per second, and 15 × 60 = 900 rpm. Same answer, just unpacked.

What the result means. The invisible magnetic pattern inside the stator circulates 900 times a minute (15 times a second). More poles means a slower field; a higher frequency means a faster one.

Sanity check. The same 60 Hz supply gives 3600 rpm (2 poles), 1800 (4), 1200 (6), 900 (8). Our 900 rpm fits that pattern. Common mistake: putting the number of pole PAIRS into p, which gives a field that is twice too fast.

AnalogyPicture a treadmill belt. The control panel (the supply frequency and how many poles the winding has) sets the belt speed, and nothing the runner does can change it. The rotating field is that belt; the rotor is the runner standing on it. ns is the belt speed, 900 rpm here.
Q2

4-pole, 50 Hz motor running at 1470 rpm. Slip?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 0.02

What it's asking. How far behind the rotating field is the rotor, written as a fraction of the field's speed? That fraction is the slip.

Steps needed. (1) Find ns from the pole count and frequency. (2) Subtract the rotor speed to get how many rpm the rotor lags. (3) Divide the lag by ns.

s = (ns − n) / ns
sslip: a pure number (no units) between 0 and 1 for a motor. 0.02 means the rotor runs 2 % slower than the field.
nssynchronous speed in rpm: the speed of the field, from 120f/p.
nactual rotor (shaft) speed in rpm, as a tachometer would read it.
ns − nthe slip speed in rpm: how many revolutions per minute the rotor loses against the field.

What it calculates: the rotor's lag as a fraction of the field speed. Why we need it: slip controls the rotor frequency, rotor current, torque and rotor copper loss, so nearly everything later is expressed in terms of s. When it is valid: an induction motor running as a motor (0 < s < 1); n and ns must be in the same units.

Step 1 — synchronous speed. ns = 120 × 50 / 4 = 1500 rpm.

Step 2 — slip speed. 1500 − 1470 = 30 rpm.

Step 3 — as a fraction of ns. s = 30 / 1500 = 0.02.

What the result means. The rotor turns 2 % slower than the field. Real induction motors at full load typically sit at 0.02 to 0.06, so 0.02 is realistic. The rotor can never reach s = 0, because with no relative motion between rotor and field nothing is induced in the rotor and there is no torque.

Sanity check. Rearrange to confirm: n = ns(1 − s) = 1500 × 0.98 = 1470 ✓. A common slip-up is dividing by the rotor speed (1470) instead of the synchronous speed (1500).

AnalogyThink of a runner chasing a pace-setter who holds exactly 1500 m/min. The runner manages 1470, so after each minute they are 30 m behind, which is 2 % of the pace-setter's distance: that is the slip. The runner must stay behind, because the ‘rope’ between them (the induced current) only pulls when it is stretched, and zero lag means a slack rope and no pull.
Q3

Slip 0.04 on a 60 Hz supply. Rotor induced frequency (Hz)?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 2.4 Hz

What it's asking. How many times per second do the currents in the rotor bars reverse direction? The rotor is not stationary, so it does not see the field sweep past at the full supply frequency.

Steps needed. (1) Note that the rotor only feels the slip speed, not the full field speed. (2) Multiply the supply frequency by the slip.

fr = s f
frfrequency of the voltage and current induced in the rotor, in Hz.
sslip as a fraction (0.04 here, not 4).
fsupply (stator) frequency in Hz: 60 Hz here.

What it calculates: how fast the rotor's EMF and current alternate. Why we need it: the rotor reactance is 2πfrL, so it shrinks as the motor speeds up, which is why X2 appears as sX2 in the real rotor. When it is valid: any induction motor; no pole count needed.

Where it comes from. A stationary rotor would see the field cross its conductors at the full rate f. A turning rotor only sees the relative speed ns − n = s·ns, which is the fraction s of that rate, so it sees s·f.

fr = 0.04 × 60 = 2.4 Hz

What the result means. The rotor currents reverse only 2.4 times per second, about one full cycle every 0.42 s: very slow, almost DC. That is normal for a running motor and is why rotor iron losses are tiny. Think of standing next to a train: when it flies past you count carriages quickly, but if you walk alongside at nearly its speed the carriages slip past slowly.

Common mistake. If the answer came out near 60 Hz you probably used s = 4 instead of 0.04. Always turn percentages into decimals first.

Q4

6-pole, 60 Hz motor at slip 3.5 %. Rotor speed (rpm)?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 1158 rpm

What it's asking. Find the actual shaft speed in rpm when you are given the slip. This is the slip formula from Q2 rearranged to solve for n.

Steps needed. (1) Get ns from 120f/p. (2) Turn 3.5 % into 0.035. (3) Multiply ns by (1 − s), the fraction of the field speed the rotor actually reaches.

n = ns(1 − s)
nrotor (shaft) speed in rpm.
nssynchronous speed in rpm.
(1 − s)the fraction of the field speed the rotor achieves. With s = 0.035 the rotor reaches 96.5 % of ns.

What it calculates: the real speed from the slip. Why we need it: the shaft speed is what the load sees, and it gives ωm for torque and power. When it is valid: motoring, 0 ≤ s ≤ 1.

Step 1 — synchronous speed. ns = 120 × 60 / 6 = 1200 rpm.

Step 2 — slip as a decimal. s = 3.5 / 100 = 0.035, so 1 − s = 0.965.

n = 1200 × 0.965 = 1158 rpm

What the result means. The shaft is 42 rpm behind the field (1200 − 1158 = 42, which equals 0.035 × 1200). A nameplate on a 6-pole 60 Hz motor typically says about 1150 to 1180 rpm, so 1158 is realistic.

Common mistake. If you got 42 rpm you multiplied by s instead of (1 − s): that is the slip speed, not the rotor speed.

Q5

Slip and rotor frequency at standstill?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: s = 1, fr = f

What it's asking. What are the slip and the rotor frequency at the instant the rotor is not turning (at start-up, or when it is held locked)?

Steps needed. (1) Set the rotor speed to zero in the slip formula. (2) Use that slip in fr = s f.

s = (ns − 0) / ns = 1     fr = s f = f
n = 0the rotor is standing still.
s = 1the largest slip possible: the rotor is lagging the field by 100 %.
frrotor frequency; at s = 1 it equals the supply frequency f.
nswhatever it is for the machine, it cancels out, so no pole count or frequency is needed.

What it calculates: the extreme end of the slip scale, the start-up or locked-rotor condition. Why we need it: starting current and starting torque are found at s = 1, and the locked-rotor test (Q28 to Q30) is done there. When it is valid: any time the rotor is not moving, whatever the pole count.

What the result means. The field sweeps past a stationary rotor at its full speed, so the rotor sees the full supply frequency and the biggest possible induced EMF. The motor acts like a transformer with a short-circuited secondary (stator = primary, rotor = secondary), which is why start-up currents are several times rated current. The slip scale runs from s = 0 (rotor at synchronous speed, no torque) to s = 1 (standstill).

Sanity check. In the equivalent circuit s = 1 makes R2/s equal to R2: only the real rotor resistance remains. Do not mix this up with s = 0, which is synchronous speed.

Write this down — Speed & slip

  • ns = 120f/p: speed of the rotating field in rpm; p is poles, not pole pairs. Always the first step.
  • s = (ns − n)/ns and n = ns(1 − s): slip is the rotor's lag as a fraction of the field speed. Convert percentages to decimals first; full-load values are typically 0.02 to 0.06.
  • fr = s f: frequency of the rotor currents. Small when running, equal to f at standstill.
  • s = 0 means synchronous speed (no torque, never reached by a motor); s = 1 means standstill (start-up and locked-rotor test).
  • Trap: the pole count goes into 120f/p as poles; slip is a pure number, not rpm; always keep n and ns in the same units.

📘 Power flow

Q6

Pag = 10 kW, s = 0.04. Rotor copper loss?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 400 W

What it's asking. Of the power crossing the air gap into the rotor, how much is simply wasted as heat in the rotor windings?

Steps needed. (1) Recognise Pag as the total power handed to the rotor. (2) Know that the fraction s of it ends up as rotor heat. (3) Multiply.

Pcu2 = s Pag
Pcu2rotor copper loss in watts: the I²R heat in the rotor conductors, equal to 3I2²R2.
sslip as a fraction (0.04).
Pagair-gap power in watts: the electrical power that crosses from the stator to the rotor, i.e. the input minus the stator copper and core losses.

What it calculates: the part of the rotor's incoming power lost as heat. Why we need it: it shows that high slip means high rotor heat, and it feeds the efficiency calculation. When it is valid: the standard induction motor model, with Pag in watts and s as a fraction; rotor iron loss is neglected because the rotor frequency is tiny.

Where it comes from. In the equivalent circuit the whole rotor branch resistance is R2/s, so Pag = 3I2²R2/s. The real copper loss is only 3I2²R2. Divide one by the other and the ratio is exactly s.

Pcu2 = 0.04 × 10 000 W = 400 W

What the result means. 4 % of the air-gap power, 0.4 kW of the 10 kW, becomes heat in the rotor. The other 96 % (9600 W) is converted to mechanical power, which is Q7.

Sanity check. Remember to work in watts (10 kW = 10 000 W), or give the answer as 0.4 kW. Pcu2 must come out much smaller than Pag at normal slip.

AnalogyAir-gap power is your gross pay packet arriving at the rotor. The slip is the tax rate: a share s goes to the taxman (heat in the rotor) and the remaining (1 − s) is your take-home pay (mechanical power). A higher slip means a heavier tax.
Q7

Pag = 20 kW, s = 0.05. Power developed?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 19000 W

What it's asking. How much of the air-gap power is actually turned into mechanical power by the rotor?

Steps needed. (1) Work out the take-home fraction (1 − s). (2) Multiply it by Pag. (3) Cross-check with Pag − Pcu2.

Pdev = (1 − s) Pag
Pdevdeveloped (internal mechanical) power in watts, equal to torque × rotor angular speed. It is before friction and windage are taken off.
(1 − s)the fraction of air-gap power that is not lost as rotor heat.
Pagair-gap power in watts.

What it calculates: the power converted from electrical to mechanical inside the machine. Why we need it: it is the link between the electrical side and the shaft, since Pout = Pdev minus friction and windage. When it is valid: always in the standard model, but Pdev is not yet the shaft output.

Step 1 — the take-home fraction. 1 − 0.05 = 0.95.

Pdev = 0.95 × 20 000 W = 19 000 W

Step 2 — cross-check. Pcu2 = 0.05 × 20 000 = 1000 W, and 19 000 + 1000 = 20 000 W = Pag ✓.

What the result means. 19 kW of mechanical power is produced inside the rotor and 1 kW is lost as heat. The rotor alone is 95 % efficient, exactly 1 − s.

Common mistake. If you got 1000 W you used s instead of (1 − s): that is the loss, not the useful part.

Q8

Pcu2 = 1.5 kW at s = 0.03. Air-gap power?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 50000 W

What it's asking. You know how much heat the rotor produces and the slip. Work backwards to the power that crossed the air gap.

Steps needed. (1) Convert 1.5 kW to 1500 W. (2) Rearrange Pcu2 = sPag to make Pag the subject. (3) Divide by the slip.

Pag = Pcu2 / s
Pagair-gap power in watts.
Pcu2rotor copper loss in watts.
sslip as a fraction (0.03).

What it calculates: the same relationship as Q6, turned around. Why we need it: rotor copper loss is often the quantity you know (from a test or from a given figure), and Pag then unlocks the torque and Pdev. When it is valid: the standard model, with both powers in the same unit.

Pag = 1500 W / 0.03 = 50 000 W

What the result means. Only 3 % of the power crossing the gap is wasted, and that 3 % is 1.5 kW, so the whole is 50 kW. Dividing by a small number gives a big number, which is the correct feel for a low-slip motor. Following on, Pdev would be 48.5 kW.

Sanity check. Multiply back: 0.03 × 50 000 = 1500 W ✓. Pag must be larger than Pcu2; if it came out smaller you multiplied instead of dividing.

Q9

4-pole, 50 Hz motor with Pag = 31.4 kW. Developed torque?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 199.9 N·m

What it's asking. What twisting force (torque) does the rotating field exert on the rotor, given the air-gap power?

Steps needed. (1) Get ns from 120f/p. (2) Convert it from rpm to rad/s. (3) Divide the air-gap power by that angular speed.

Tdev = Pag / ωs     ωs = 2π ns / 60
Tdevdeveloped torque in N·m: the twisting effort the field puts on the rotor.
Pagair-gap power in watts.
ωssynchronous angular speed in rad/s: the field's speed in the unit the physics formulas need.
2πradians in one revolution.
60seconds in a minute, to turn revolutions per MINUTE into per SECOND.

What it calculates: torque from power ÷ angular speed (P = Tω). Why use the synchronous speed: the field turns at ωs and it is the air-gap power that the field delivers to the rotor. When it is valid: always; but you must use rad/s, never rpm.

Step 1 — angular speed. ns = 120 × 50 / 4 = 1500 rpm, so ωs = 2π × 1500 / 60 = 157.08 rad/s.

Tdev = 31 400 / 157.08 = 199.9 N·m

Why this is the same as Pdev/ωm. Using Pdev and the real rotor speed gives the same torque: Pdev = (1 − s)Pag and ωm = (1 − s)ωs, so the factor (1 − s) cancels. That is why Pag/ωs is the easy route when s is unknown.

What the result means. About 200 N·m, roughly the twist you get by hanging a 20 kg mass on a 1 m spanner (20 × 9.81 ≈ 196 N·m).

Common mistake. If your torque is nonsensical by a factor of about 9.5 you probably left the speed in rpm.

Q10

Pout = 15 kW at 1440 rpm. Load torque?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 99.5 N·m

What it's asking. What torque does the load actually feel at the output shaft? This is the useful torque, as opposed to the internally developed torque of Q9.

Steps needed. (1) Convert the shaft speed from rpm to rad/s. (2) Divide the output power by it.

Tload = Pout / ωm     ωm = 2π n / 60
Tloadshaft (load) torque in N·m.
Poutmechanical power available at the shaft in watts, after friction and windage losses.
ωmactual rotor angular speed in rad/s, from the real speed n (1440 rpm), not from ns.
nactual shaft speed in rpm.

What it calculates: the torque delivered to the pump, fan or conveyor. Why we need it: this is what the motor must supply to the mechanical load. When it is valid: any P = Tω situation, with the SHAFT speed (not the synchronous speed) because Pout is measured at the shaft.

Step 1 — angular speed. ωm = 2π × 1440 / 60 = 150.80 rad/s.

Tload = 15 000 / 150.80 = 99.47 ≈ 99.5 N·m

Sanity check. Multiply back: 99.47 × 150.80 = 15 000 W ✓. Rule of thumb: T ≈ 9.55 P / n = 9.55 × 15 000 / 1440 = 99.5 N·m. Using ωs = 157.08 instead of ωm would give 95.5 N·m, which is the wrong speed for the wrong power.

What the result means. The shaft delivers about 99.5 N·m. The developed torque is slightly larger, because some torque is eaten by friction and windage before it reaches the shaft.

Q11

440 V line, 30 A, pf 0.8 lagging. Input power?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 18290 W

What it's asking. How much real electrical power (the part that does work and makes heat, not the reactive part) does the motor draw from the supply?

Steps needed. (1) Multiply √3 by the line voltage and line current to get the apparent power. (2) Multiply by the power factor to keep only the real part.

Pin = √3 VL IL cos φ
Pinreal (active) input power in watts, three phases together.
VLline-to-line voltage in volts: the figure on the nameplate (440 V).
ILcurrent in one supply line in amps (30 A).
cos φpower factor: the fraction of the apparent power that is real. ‘Lagging’ means the current lags the voltage, which is normal for an inductive motor.
√3comes from three phases each with VL/√3: 3 × (VL/√3) × I = √3 VLI.

What it calculates: the total electrical power in. Why we need it: it is the start of the power chain (Pin → Pag → Pdev → Pout) and the denominator of efficiency. When it is valid: a balanced three-phase load, Y or delta, provided VL and IL are LINE values.

Step 1 — √3 × VL. √3 × 440 = 762.10 V.

Step 2 — × IL. 762.10 × 30 = 22 863 VA, the apparent power S.

Pin = 22 863 × 0.8 = 18 290 W (about 18.29 kW)

What the result means. The supply provides 22.86 kVA, but only 18.29 kW is real work and heat; the rest, 22 863 × 0.6 ≈ 13.7 kvar, is reactive power that builds the magnetic field and goes back and forth.

Common mistake. Use cos φ, not sin φ, for real power. Using 3 instead of √3 would give 31.7 kW, an impossible overshoot of the 22.86 kVA that was supplied.

Q12

I = 30 A, R1 = 0.2 Ω/phase. Stator copper loss?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 540 W

What it's asking. How much heat is generated in the three stator windings?

Steps needed. (1) Find the I²R heat of one phase. (2) Multiply by 3 because there are three phase windings.

Pcu1 = 3 I² R1
Pcu1stator copper loss in watts.
3the three phase windings, each dissipating the same amount.
Icurrent in ONE phase winding, in amps (30 A). In a Y connection the phase current equals the line current.
R1resistance of one stator phase winding in ohms (0.2 Ω).

What it calculates: the I²R heating in the stator wire. Why we need it: it is the first deduction on the way from Pin to Pag: Pag = Pin − Pcu1 − Pcore. When it is valid: I must be the phase-winding current (for a delta connection use IL/√3).

Q13

Pout = 28 700 W, Pin = 32 389 W. Efficiency?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 88.6 %

What it's asking. What fraction of the electrical power going in comes out as useful mechanical power?

Steps needed. (1) Divide output by input in the same units. (2) Multiply by 100 for a percentage.

η = Pout / Pin
ηefficiency: a pure number below 1, or a percentage after × 100.
Poutuseful mechanical power at the shaft in watts (28 700 W).
Pinelectrical input power in watts (32 389 W).

What it calculates: the headline performance score of the motor. Why we need it: it tells you how much of what you pay for is wasted. When it is valid: both powers measured at the same operating point and in the same units; it is always less than 100 %.

η = 28 700 / 32 389 = 0.8861 = 88.6 %

Q14

Why is the quantity R2(1−s)/s special? Evaluate for R2 = 0.2 Ω, s = 0.04.

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 4.8 Ω

What it's asking. Why does the combination R2(1 − s)/s keep appearing in the equivalent circuit, and what is its value here? In short: it is an electrical stand-in for the mechanical load.

Steps needed. (1) Split the rotor-branch resistance R2/s into two pieces. (2) See which piece accounts for heat and which for mechanical power. (3) Evaluate it.

R2/s = R2 + R2(1 − s)/s     Pdev = 3 I2² R2(1 − s)/s
R2real rotor winding resistance per phase, referred to the stator side, in ohms.
sslip as a fraction.
I2rotor current per phase (referred to the stator) in amps.
R2(1 − s)/sthe equivalent load resistance in ohms: a fictitious resistor whose I²R power stands for the mechanical power.

What it calculates: a resistor that dissipates exactly the mechanical power. Why we need it: it lets one ordinary AC circuit describe both the electrical and mechanical sides, with the load shown as a resistance. When it is valid: the per-phase equivalent circuit of the induction motor.

Why it is special. Power in the whole branch R2/s: 3I2²R2/s = Pag. Power in the real R2: 3I2²R2 = sPag = Pcu2. What is left over: 3I2²(R2/s − R2) = 3I2²R2(1 − s)/s = (1 − s)Pag = Pdev. So the leftover piece is exactly the mechanical power.

R2(1 − s)/s = 0.2 × 0.96 / 0.04 = 0.192 / 0.04 = 4.8 Ω

What the result means. At light load s is tiny, this resistor is huge, little current flows. As the shaft load rises, the motor slips more, the resistor shrinks, and more current and more mechanical power flow. At s = 1 (standstill) it is 0 Ω: the secondary is shorted and there is no mechanical output.

Sanity check. 0.2 Ω + 4.8 Ω = 5.0 Ω = R2/s ✓, which is the answer to Q16.

AnalogyIt is a rheostat (variable resistor) controlled by the shaft load. Turn the knob to a lower resistance and the battery supplies more current and more power: loading the shaft turns the knob down.

Write this down — Power flow

  • Pin = √3 VLIL cos φ: real power drawn from the supply. Use LINE values; √3, not 3.
  • Pcu1 = 3I²R1: stator copper loss. The 3 is for the three phase windings.
  • Power chain: Pin − Pcu1 − Pcore = Pag; Pag − Pcu2 = Pdev; Pdev − friction & windage = Pout.
  • Pcu2 = sPag, Pdev = (1 − s)Pag, Pag = Pcu2/s. So Pag : Pcu2 : Pdev = 1 : s : (1 − s).
  • Torque: Tdev = Pag/ωs = Pdev/ωm; Tload = Pout/ωm. Always ω = 2πn/60 in rad/s, never rpm.
  • η = Pout/Pin. R2/s = R2 + R2(1 − s)/s, and the second piece is the ‘load resistor’ for mechanical power.

Q15

Phase voltage of a 3300 V (line) Y-connected motor?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 1905.3 V

What it's asking. The nameplate gives the line-to-line voltage. What voltage actually sits across one winding of a Y-connected motor?

Steps needed. (1) Note the connection is Y (star). (2) Divide the line voltage by √3.

Vφ = VL / √3
Vφphase voltage in volts: the voltage across a single winding, and the source voltage of the per-phase equivalent circuit.
VLline-to-line voltage in volts (3300 V).
√3 ≈ 1.732line voltage is the difference of two phase voltages that are 120° apart, and that difference has magnitude √3 times one phase voltage.

What it calculates: the voltage the per-phase circuit is driven by. Why we need it: the equivalent circuit models ONE phase (the other two are identical copies), so it must be fed the phase voltage. When it is valid: ONLY for a Y connection; for a delta connection Vφ = VL.

Vφ = 3300 / 1.7321 = 1905.26 ≈ 1905.3 V

What the result means. Each winding sees about 58 % of the line voltage; the star point sits at the centre.

AnalogyAnalysing one phase and multiplying by three is like studying one lane of a three-lane motorway where every lane carries identical traffic: you only need to understand one lane, then scale up. Vφ is the ‘lane voltage’.
Q16

R2 = 0.2 Ω at s = 0.04. What is R2/s?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: 5.0 Ω

What it's asking. In the per-phase equivalent circuit, what resistance does the rotor branch present to the stator?

Steps needed. (1) Take the real rotor resistance R2. (2) Divide by the slip.

R2 / s
R2/seffective rotor-branch resistance in ohms, as seen from the stator side.
R2real rotor winding resistance per phase, referred to the stator (0.2 Ω).
sslip as a fraction (0.04).

What it calculates: the rotor resistance after the slip has been folded into it. Why we need it: the real rotor sits at frequency sf with impedance R2 + jsX2; dividing everything by s gives R2/s + jX2 at the supply frequency, so the rotor can sit in the same circuit as the stator. When it is valid: the standard per-phase equivalent circuit, with R2 referred to the stator.

R2/s = 0.2 / 0.04 = 5.0 Ω

What the result means. The rotor looks 25 times more resistive than its real wire (5.0 Ω against 0.2 Ω). Steps needed. (1) Disconnect the rotor branch, leaving two open terminals across jXM. (2) Vth = the voltage across those open terminals, found by a voltage divider. (3) Zth = the impedance looking back into the terminals with the source Vφ shorted: R1 + jX1 in parallel with jXM.

Zth = (R1 + jX1) ∥ jXM = jXM(R1 + jX1) / (R1 + j(X1 + XM))
VthThevenin voltage: the open-circuit voltage the rotor branch would see if it were unplugged.

Q19

Formula for smax and Tmax?

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: smax = R2/√(Rth²+(Xth+X2)²); Tmax = 3Vth²/(2ωs[Rth+√(Rth²+(Xth+X2)²)])

What it's asking. At what slip does the motor produce its largest (pull-out) torque, and how large is that torque? Both are found by treating the torque as a power-transfer problem.

Steps needed. (1) Use the Thevenin circuit to get the rotor current. (2) Torque = power into the R2/s resistor ÷ ωs. (3) That power is largest when R2/s equals the magnitude of the rest of the loop (maximum power transfer). (4) Solve for s, then put it back in to get Tmax.

I2 = Vth / [ (Rth + R2/s) + j(Xth + X2) ]     T = 3 I2² (R2/s) / ωs
I2rotor current per phase (referred to the stator) in amps.
Vth, Rth, XthThevenin voltage, resistance and reactance from Q18.
R2/s, X2rotor-branch resistance and leakage reactance.
3the three phases.
ωssynchronous speed in rad/s: torque = air-gap power ÷ ωs.

What it calculates: the peak (pull-out or breakdown) torque and the slip at which it happens. Why we need it: beyond the peak the motor cannot carry the load and stalls, so it tells you the overload limit. When it is valid: the Thevenin circuit at constant V and f.

Maximum power transfer. Torque is proportional to the power in the R2/s resistor, which is the one part of the circuit that changes with slip. Everything else forms a fixed source impedance Zs = Rth + j(Xth + X2). A resistor RL behind a fixed source impedance receives the most power when RL = |Zs|. It cannot cancel the reactance (it is a pure resistance), so it matches the magnitude, not just the resistance.

R2/smax = √(Rth² + (Xth + X2)²) ⇒ smax = R2 / √(Rth² + (Xth + X2)²)

Peak torque. Put RL = |Zs| into the torque. With |Zs|² = Rth² + (Xth + X2)², the loop impedance squared (Rth + |Zs|)² + (Xth + X2)² simplifies to 2|Zs|(|Zs| + Rth). One factor |Zs| cancels with the numerator, leaving the formula below.

Tmax = 3Vth² / ( 2ωs [ Rth + √(Rth² + (Xth + X2)²) ] )

What the result means. smax grows with R2; Tmax does not contain R2, which is exactly why Q20 is false. Tmax grows with Vth², which gives Q21. The 3 is the three phases and the 2 comes from the simplification above. I also checked numerically, sweeping s with a test circuit: the largest torque occurred at the formula's smax and equalled the formula's Tmax.

Common mistake. ωs must be in rad/s, and do not forget the 3.

AnalogyA battery has a little internal resistance. Connect a very small load and the current is huge but the voltage collapses across the internal resistance; connect a very large load and the voltage is fine but the current is tiny. The most power goes into the load when its size matches the battery's own internal impedance. The slip is the knob that changes the load R2/s, and the pull-out torque is the point where the match is best.

Q31

A 3-phase, 6-pole, 50 Hz induction motor runs at 960 rpm and takes 25 kW from the supply. The stator copper loss is 900 W; neglect core loss. Find the slip, the air-gap power, the rotor copper loss and the power developed.

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: s = 0.04; Pag = 24 100 W; Pcu2 = 964 W; Pdev = 23 136 W

What it's asking. Electrical power goes in at the terminals and some of it is lost as heat on the way to the shaft. Four numbers are wanted: how far the rotor lags the field (slip), how much power crosses the air gap into the rotor, how much of that is wasted as heat in the rotor bars, and how much is turned into mechanical power.

Steps needed. (1) Synchronous speed from the pole count and frequency, then slip from the actual speed. (2) Take the stator copper loss off the input to get the air-gap power. (3) Rotor copper loss is the slip fraction of the air-gap power. (4) What is left of the air-gap power is the power developed.

nssynchronous speed in rpm: the speed of the rotating magnetic field.
nactual rotor speed in rpm (960).

Pinelectrical power drawn from the supply, in watts (25 000 W).

Pdevpower developed: the share of the air-gap power converted into mechanical form (before friction and windage are deducted).

What it calculates: the first half of the power chain, from the supply down to the mechanical power inside the rotor. Why this order: slip is needed for the rotor loss, and Pag is the only power that the slip splits (Q6 and Q7 do the same split in isolation). When it is valid: any induction motor; here core loss is assumed zero.

Step 1 — speed and slip. ns = 120 × 50 / 6 = 1000 rpm, so

s = (1000 − 960) / 1000 = 0.04   (4 %)

Step 2 — air-gap power.

Step 3 — rotor copper loss.

Step 4 — power developed.

Pdev = (1 − 0.04) × 24 100 = 0.96 × 24 100 = 23 136 W

Sanity check. The two routes to Pdev agree. Also Pag : Pcu2 : Pdev = 1 : 0.04 : 0.96, and 964 + 23 136 = 24 100 ✓. A typical motor loses only a few percent in the rotor at full load, which matches s = 4 %.

That department takes a levy equal to the slip, 4 % of 24 100 = 964, as heat; the remaining 96 % (23 136) is real ‘spendable’ mechanical power. Q32

The same motor (3-phase, 6-pole, 50 Hz, running at 960 rpm, Pin = 25 kW, Pcu1 = 900 W, no core loss). The rotational (friction + windage) losses are 500 W. Find the output power, the efficiency and the load torque (also state the developed torque).

ωssynchronous angular speed (of the field), in rad/s.

Step 2 — efficiency.

Write this down — Tutorial 8 power chain

  • Chain: Pin → (− Pcu1, − Pcore) → Pag → (− Pcu2 = sPag) → Pdev = (1 − s)Pag → (− Prot) → Pout. Work down it one subtraction at a time.

Answer: Tst = 97.06 N·m; Rext = 1.504 Ω; Tst becomes 222.3 N·m (= Tmax)

What it's asking. At the instant of switching on, the rotor is stationary and the torque is the starting torque. With the rotor winding as it is, how big is it? And how much extra resistance in the rotor circuit would make the motor start with the biggest torque it can possibly produce?

Steps needed. (1) Put s = 1 in the torque formula, so R2/s = R2. (2) For the best start, the peak must sit at s = 1, so R2 + Rext = D. (3) Solve for Rext. (4) Recalculate the starting torque with the new resistance.

What it's asking. Torque and mechanical power are different things. The motor produces its greatest torque at 1185 rpm (Q37). At what speed is the mechanical power (what the motor actually ‘delivers’ in watts) the greatest, and how much is it?

Steps needed. (1) Split the rotor resistance R2/s into R2 + RL, where RL = R2(1 − s)/s is the ‘load resistor’ whose power is Pdev (Q14). When it is valid: constant V and f, same Thevenin loop.

Step 1 — optimum load resistance. Rth + R2 = 0.2869 + 0.4 = 0.6869 Ω and Xth + X2 = 1.8823 Ω.

The product therefore keeps climbing for a while after the torque peak and tops out at the lower slip of 0.1664 (higher speed 1250 rpm). Numbers: at the torque peak, T = 222.3 N·m at ωm = 124.08 rad/s gives 27.59 kW. At the power peak, T = 217.2 N·m (28 437 / 130.94) at ωm = 130.94 rad/s gives 28.44 kW. AnalogyA car engine: its maximum torque occurs at a modest engine speed, but its maximum power occurs at a higher speed, because power is torque multiplied by speed and the speed gain outweighs the small torque fall. The motor behaves the same: the torque peak is at 1185 rpm and the power peak at 1250 rpm. (The ‘match the source’ rule is the battery-internal-resistance idea from Q19, now applied to RL rather than R2/s.)

Q41

A no-load test on a 3-phase, 400 V (line), 50 Hz, Y-connected induction motor gives 400 V, 6 A and 300 W. A DC test gave R1 = 0.3 Ω per phase and the locked-rotor test gave X1 = X2 = 0.9 Ω. Find the magnetising reactance XM and the rotational losses (use the course's approximation that the no-load circuit is X1 + XM in series, ignoring the small no-load resistance).

Try it yourself first, then reveal the full solution
Answer: XM ≈ 37.59 Ω; Prot = 267.6 W

What it's asking. Run the motor with no load on the shaft at full voltage and measure the current and the power it takes. That gives the last unknowns of the equivalent circuit: how much current is needed to set up the magnetic field (the magnetising reactance), and how much power the motor wastes just spinning itself.

Steps needed. (1) Convert the line voltage to phase voltage. (2) Divide by the phase current to get the no-load impedance. (3) Subtract X1 to leave XM. (4) Take the stator copper loss out of the measured power; what remains is the rotational loss.

|Znl| = Vφ / Inl ≈ X1 + XM    XM = |Znl| − X1    Prot = Pnl − 3Inl²R1
Vφphase voltage, VL/√3 = 230.94 V.
Inlno-load line current (= phase current in a Y), 6 A.
|Znl|magnitude of the impedance seen per phase at no load, ohms.
X1stator leakage reactance, 0.9 Ω (from the locked-rotor test).
XMmagnetising reactance, ohms.
Pnltotal three-phase power read on the wattmeter, 300 W.
R1stator resistance per phase, 0.3 Ω (from the DC test, Q27).
Protrotational losses: friction, windage (and, in this approximation, any core loss), in watts.

What it calculates: the two parameters that cannot come from the DC and locked-rotor tests. Why the shortcut works: at no load the rotor spins at almost synchronous speed, so s ≈ 0 and R2/s becomes huge. A huge impedance in parallel with jXM carries almost no current, so the rotor branch is effectively open-circuited and all the current goes through X1 and XM in series. When it is valid: induction motors with a small no-load slip, tested at rated voltage.

Step 1 — phase voltage. 400 / 1.7321 = 230.94 V.

Step 2 — no-load impedance.

|Znl| = 230.94 / 6 = 38.49 Ω

Step 3 — magnetising reactance.

XM = 38.49 − 0.9 = 37.59 Ω

Step 4 — rotational losses. At no load the rotor takes almost no current, so the only electrical loss is the stator copper loss:

3Inl²R1 = 3 × 6² × 0.3 = 3 × 36 × 0.3 = 32.4 W
Prot = 300 − 32.4 = 267.6 W

What the result means. Of the 300 W drawn, only 32.4 W heats the stator. The remaining 267.6 W is the motor’s own ‘cost of spinning’: friction in the bearings and air drag, which the supply must cover even when the motor is delivering nothing. XM = 37.6 Ω is large, as expected. The magnetising reactance has to be large compared with the leakage reactances, or the no-load current would be a big fraction of the full-load current.

The small approximation. The wattmeter reading also tells us a no-load resistance Rnl = P/(3I²) = 300 / 108 = 2.78 Ω. If it were included exactly, Xnl = √(38.49² − 2.78²) = 38.39 Ω, so XM = 37.49 Ω. The difference from 37.59 Ω is 0.1 Ω (0.3 %), which is why the course ignores it.

Common mistake. Using 400 V with 6 A (66.7 Ω, an impedance that is too large by √3), or reporting all 300 W as rotational loss without subtracting the stator copper loss.

AnalogyA car idling in neutral on a level road. It goes nowhere, yet it still burns a little fuel, just to overcome its own internal friction and keep the engine turning. The 300 W at no load is that idling fuel: a small amount goes to heat in the windings, and the rest overcomes the motor’s bearing friction and air drag.
