Concept 0 of 10

How it works: the rotating field and the two rotor types

A 3-phase stator carries currents ia = Imaxsinωt, ib = Imaxsin(ωt−120°), ic = Imaxsin(ωt−240°) in three coils spaced 120° apart. Each coil makes a pulsating field; added up they give one field of constant magnitude 1.5Bmax that rotates at synchronous speed. The rotor winding is short-circuited, so the rotating field induces a rotor emf (Faraday), a rotor current flows, and the interaction of that current with the field produces torque. No electrical connection to the rotor: it is a rotating transformer.

Squirrel cagebars shorted by end rings; rugged, cheap, no access to the rotor circuit.
Wound rotor3-phase rotor winding brought out through slip rings and brushes, so external resistance can be added (the ‘Rext’ in the exam questions).
AnalogySpin a horseshoe magnet under a copper-bottomed pan on a pivot: the pan is dragged round but always a little slower than the magnet. The pan only feels a pull because it is lagging; if it matched the speed, there would be no relative motion, no induced current and no pull.

Why slip can never be zero: at synchronous speed the flux would not cut the rotor bars, so the rotor emf, current and torque would fall to zero, and friction would slow the rotor down. Changing the supply frequency scales synchronous speed: e.g. a 6-pole, 60 Hz motor at 85 % of rated frequency has ns = 120(60)(0.85)/6 = 1020 rpm (Lecture 1).

Lecture 1 Example 2: 208 V, 10 hp, 4-pole, 60 Hz, Y-connected, full-load slip 5 %

Concept 1 of 7

The rotating field, synchronous speed, slip & rotor frequency

Three-phase currents in the stator create a magnetic field that rotates at the synchronous speed. The rotor (a short-circuited winding) is dragged round by that field, but it always lags a little: if it caught up exactly, nothing would cut the rotor conductors, no emf and no torque would arise. That lag is the slip.

ns = 120 f / p   |   s = (ns − n) / ns   |   fr = s f   |   ω = 2πn/60
fsupply frequency in Hz (50 or 60).
pnumber of poles (a 4-pole machine has 2 pairs). More poles = slower rotating field.
nsspeed of the rotating field in rpm (= 1500 for 4-pole/50 Hz, 1000 for 6-pole/50 Hz, 1200 for 6-pole/60 Hz).
nactual rotor speed (rpm), always slightly below ns for a motor.
sslip as a fraction (0.03 = 3 %). At standstill s = 1; at synchronous speed s = 0.
frfrequency of the voltage induced in the rotor: the rotor sees the field sweeping past at the slip speed, so fr = s·f.
AnalogyA treadmill belt (the rotating field) moves at 10 km/h and you jog on it at 9.65 km/h. You drift backwards relative to the belt at 0.35 km/h: that is the slip speed. The frequency of the rotor’s emf is how often the rotor ‘sees’ a field pole pass, which is tiny when slip is small (1–5 % of f).
Tutorial 8 Q1: 220 V, 3-phase, 6-pole, 50 Hz, slip 3.5 %
  1. (a) ns = 120(50)/6 = 1000 rpm. (b) n = ns(1−s) = 1000(0.965) = 965 rpm.
  2. (c) slip speed = ns − n = 35 rpm. (d) fr = sf = 0.035(50) = 1.75 Hz.

A 3-phase, 50-Hz, 6-pole induction motor has stator copper loss 800 W and rotational loss 1000 W. (i) At 950 rpm with a load torque of 180 N·m, find the power developed and the efficiency. (ii) If the frequency of the voltage induced in the rotor is 2 Hz, find the motor speed.

Try it yourself first, then reveal the solution
Solution

Formulae: ns = 120f/p; s = (ns−n)/ns; Pout = Tload·ωm; Pdev = Pout + Prot; Pag = Pdev/(1−s); Pin = Pag + Pcu1; fr = sf.

  1. ns = 120(50)/6 = 1000 rpm; s = (1000−950)/1000 = 0.05; ωm = 2π(950)/60 = 99.48 rad/s.
  2. Pout = 180 × 99.48 = 17 907 W (load torque is the shaft torque, after rotational losses).
  3. Pdev = Pout + Prot = 17 907 + 1000 = 18 907 W.
  4. Pag = 18 907/(1−0.05) = 19 902 W; Pin = 19 902 + 800 = 20 702 W.
  5. η = 17 907/20 702 = 86.5 %.
  6. (ii) fr = s·f ⇒ s = 2/50 = 0.04 ⇒ n = ns(1−s) = 1000(0.96) = 960 rpm.
Concept 2 of 7

Power flow & torque: where does every watt go?

Pin → (−Pcu1) → Pag → (−Pcu2) → Pdev → (−Prot) → Pout
Pinelectrical input = √3VLILcosφ (or 3VφIφcosφ).
Pcu1stator copper loss = 3I²R1 (core loss is neglected in this course’s equivalent circuit).
Pagair-gap power: what crosses to the rotor = Pin − Pcu1 = 3I2²R2/s.
Pcu2rotor copper loss = sPag = 3I2²R2.
Pdevmechanical power developed = (1−s)Pag = Tdevωm.
Protrotational losses (friction + windage), subtracted to get Pout = Tloadωm.

Torque: Tdev = Pdev/ωm = Pag/ωs (ωs = 2πns/60). Load (shaft) torque Tload = Pout/ωm. Key ratio: Pcu2 : Pdev : Pag = s : (1−s) : 1. Efficiency η = Pout/Pin.

AnalogyA pay cheque: gross (Pin) → first tax (stator copper) → what actually reaches the rotor (Pag) → a slip-sized ‘fee’ s lost as heat in the rotor → the rest, 1−s, becomes mechanical power → minus friction = what you take home (Pout). A motor with big slip wastes a big fraction in the rotor.

Concept 3 of 7

The per-phase equivalent circuit

Where R2/s comes from (Lecture 2): the rotor emf and reactance both scale with the rotor frequency, so at slip s they are ER = sER0 and XR = sXR0 (R0 = locked-rotor value). The rotor current is IR = sER0/(RR+jsXR0) = ER0/(RR/s + jXR0), so the whole slip effect is concentrated in the resistor RR/s. Referring to the stator with the effective turns ratio aeff: R2 = aeff²RR, X2 = aeff²XR0. Check (Lecture 2 Ex 1): a 6-pole wound rotor with 60 Hz and 100 V at standstill, running at 1100 rpm: s = 0.0833, fr = 5 Hz, ER = 8.33 V. Core loss RC is lumped with friction, windage and stray into the rotational loss, so RC is left out of the circuit.

Analyse one phase (Vφ = VL/√3 for Y). The rotor is replaced by its stator-referred equivalent whose resistor depends on slip:

Zin = R1 + jX1 + [ jXM ∥ (R2/s + jX2) ]    I1 = Vφ/Zin    pf = cos(arg Zin)
R1, X1stator winding resistance and leakage reactance.
XMmagnetising reactance (the branch that makes the air-gap flux); core loss is ignored.
R2, X2rotor resistance and leakage reactance referred to the stator.
R2/sone resistor that bundles the rotor loss and the mechanical load: R2/s = R2 + R2(1−s)/s. The second term is the ‘load resistor’ that represents Pdev.
  1. Find s from the speed; compute R2/s.
  2. Parallel combination of jXM with (R2/s + jX2), add R1 + jX1 ⇒ Zin.
  3. I1 = Vφ/Zin; Pin = 3I1²Re(Zin); Pcu1 = 3I1²R1; Pag = Pin − Pcu1; then the flow in concept 2.
AnalogyAt standstill (s = 1) R2/s is small: the rotor looks like a short-circuited transformer, so the motor draws a huge current. As it speeds up, R2/s grows, the rotor branch becomes high-impedance and the current falls to a modest running value.

Concept 4 of 7

Thevenin equivalent: maximum torque and the slip where it occurs

To get the torque curve cleanly, replace everything to the left of the rotor branch (source, R1, X1, XM) by its Thevenin equivalent seen from the rotor terminals.

Vth = VφXM/|R1+j(X1+XM)|    Zth = jXM(R1+jX1)/(R1+j(X1+XM)) = Rth + jXth
Tdev(s) = 3Vth²(R2/s) / {ωs[(Rth+R2/s)² + (Xth+X2)²]}
smax = R2 / √(Rth²+(Xth+X2)²)    Tmax = 3Vth² / {2ωs[Rth + √(Rth²+(Xth+X2)²)]}
Vth, Zththe source voltage and series impedance seen by the rotor branch (a voltage divider between XM and the stator impedance).
smaxslip at peak torque. Notice it is proportional to R2, while Tmax does not depend on R2 at all.
Tmaxpull-out (breakdown) torque, ∝ Vth². A 10 % sag in voltage costs ~19 % of the peak torque.

The torque–speed curve has three regions (Lecture 4): motoring (0 < s < 1: rotor turns the same way as the field, slower than it; T = 0 at ns, starting torque slightly above full-load torque, peak = pull-out torque); generating (s < 0: an external prime mover drives the rotor above ns and the torque reverses; used in wind turbines); plugging/braking (s > 1: rotor turning against the field after swapping two stator phases, which stops the motor rapidly).

AnalogyClimbing a hill on a bike: there is a gear (slip) where you pull hardest, and beyond that point pedalling harder in a lower gear just wastes energy. Past smax the motor stalls (torque falls as slip rises), so operation is normally on the stable low-slip side.

Concept 5 of 7

Rotor resistance: changing the starting torque

Because smax ∝ R2, adding resistance to the rotor circuit (slip-ring motors) slides the whole torque curve towards higher slip while leaving Tmax unchanged.

Maximum torque at starting ⇔ smax = 1 ⇔ R2 + Rext = √(Rth²+(Xth+X2)²)

Starting torque (s = 1): Tst = 3Vth²R2,tot / {ωs[(Rth+R2,tot)²+(Xth+X2)²]} where R2,tot = R2+Rext. With R1 = X1 = 0 this simplifies to Tst ∝ R2,tot/(R2,tot²+X2²).

AnalogyStarting a heavy car in a high gear stalls it; adding the ‘rotor resistance’ is like shifting to a lower gear for the launch: you get strong pull at near-zero speed, then you short out the resistors (shift up) to run efficiently at low slip.

Concept 6 of 7

Shortcuts when R1 = 0 (and X1 = 0)

Several finals set R1 = X1 = 0 (‘negligible stator impedance’) so that you can skip the Thevenin algebra: Rth = 0, Xth = 0, Vth = Vφ.

smax = R2/X2    Tmax/T = (smax² + s²)/(2 smax s)    Tst ∝ R2/(R2²+X2²)

The ratio formula needs no voltage or ωs because they cancel (derive it: T = K(R2/s)/[(R2/s)²+X2²] with smax = R2/X2). Tutorial 10 Q2 is the same idea: multiply R2 by 5 and slip by 5 ⇒ R2/s unchanged ⇒ same rotor current and same torque (345 N·m), but rotor copper loss Pcu2 = sPag becomes 5×5.69 = 28.4 kW.

Tutorial 10 Q3: show Tmax/T = (smax²+s²)/(2smaxs) for R1 = 0
  1. With R1 = 0: Rth = 0, Xth = X1XM/(X1+XM), so let X = Xth+X2. T = K(R2/s)/[(R2/s)²+X²] and smax = R2/X.
  2. Write R2 = smaxX: T = K(smaxX/s)/[X²(smax/s)²+X²] = (K/X)·(smax/s)/[(smax/s)²+1] = (K/X)·smaxs/(smax²+s²).
  3. At s = smax: Tmax = (K/X)·1/2. Dividing: Tmax/T = (smax²+s²)/(2smaxs) ✓.

Concept 8 of 10

Speed control and starting

Since n = (1−s)ns and ns = 120f/p, speed is changed either by changing the slip or by changing the synchronous speed.

MethodHowComment
Rotor resistance (wound rotor)raise R2 ⇒ smax rises, Tmax unchangedinefficient (extra rotor copper loss)
Terminal voltageT ∝ V²limited speed range
Number of polesns = 120f/p, needs multiple stator windingsstepped speeds, costly
Supply frequency (V/f control)vary f; below base speed reduce V in proportion to fmost common; keeping V/f constant keeps flux φ ≈ V/(4.44N f) constant, otherwise the core saturates and IM becomes huge

Starting: direct-on-line draws about 5–6× rated current (see the 5.7× in Tutorial 10), dipping the supply. Remedies: star–delta starting (start in Y so the phase voltage is V/√3, run in Δ), autotransformer to reduce the voltage, series inductors/resistors (rare today), or for wound rotors extra rotor resistance, which both raises the starting torque and lowers the starting current.

AnalogyStar–delta starting is like starting a car in a low gear: gentle, low current, then change to the full-voltage connection once the motor is up to speed.
