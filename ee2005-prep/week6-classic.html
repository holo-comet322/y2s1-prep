Why a real transformer isn't the "ideal" transformer from Week 5

Last week's ideal transformer assumed a wire with zero resistance, a core that needs zero current to magnetise it, and a core that loses zero energy to heat. None of that is true for an actual physical transformer. This week is entirely about patching those three lies into the model, one non-ideal effect at a time, until we arrive at a circuit you can actually calculate with — first the "exact" version, then a simplified "approximate" version that's what real exam/tutorial questions expect you to draw and use.

The first, most obvious non-ideality: real copper wire has resistance. Both the primary winding (resistance R₁) and the secondary winding (resistance R₂) are physical coils of wire, and any current flowing through them dissipates power as heat (I₁²R₁ and I₂²R₂) and causes a voltage drop (I₁R₁ and I₂R₂) exactly like any resistor would. Lec 8 · p.8

The fix in the model is simple: draw R₁ and R₂ as ordinary lumped resistors sitting outside the (otherwise still-ideal) transformer core, one in series with each winding. This immediately means the terminal voltage V is no longer equal to the internally-induced EMF E — some voltage gets "used up" crossing the resistor before it even reaches the ideal core. So V₁ ≠ E₁, and V₂ ≠ E₂. Lec 8 · p.9

Analogy Think of the ideal transformer's core as a perfect water pump sitting in the middle of a pipe run. The wires leading to it aren't perfect pipes though — they have friction (resistance). Turn on the tap (apply V₁) and some of your pressure is lost just pushing water through the pipe (I₁R₁) before it ever reaches the pump. What the pump itself "feels" (E₁) is always a bit less than what you supplied at the tap (V₁).

Leakage flux → leakage reactance X₁, X₂

Second non-ideality: in an ideal transformer, every flux line created by the primary links the secondary too (a single mutual flux Φₘ). In reality, some flux lines set up by the primary current close their loop through the air near the winding without ever cutting through the secondary coil, and vice-versa. These are called leakage flux, Φ_l1 (primary-only) and Φ_l2 (secondary-only). Lec 8 · p.10

A flux that links only one winding still induces a voltage in that same winding (Faraday's Law doesn't care whether the flux is "useful" mutual flux or "wasted" leakage flux) — and since this leakage flux is proportional to the winding's own current, it behaves exactly like an extra series inductor. So it's modelled as a leakage reactance: jX₁ in series with the primary, jX₂ in series with the secondary, right alongside R₁ and R₂. Lec 8 · p.11

Analogy Picture two people passing a rope back and forth to signal each other (mutual flux — a shared, useful link). Leakage flux is like each person also idly swinging some slack rope around on their own side that never reaches the other person — it still costs them energy/effort (it still induces a voltage, since it's still changing flux linking that coil), but it does nothing useful for communication between the two sides. That "wasted, self-only" effort is exactly what X₁ and X₂ represent.

Putting the two series non-idealities together

With R₁, jX₁ in series with the primary and R₂, jX₂ in series with the secondary, the relationship between the winding's own terminal voltage and its own internal EMF becomes:

V₁ = E₁ + I₁(R₁ + jX₁)        E₂ = V₂ + I₂(R₂ + jX₂)
V₁, V₂the actual terminal voltages you'd measure with a meter at the primary/secondary terminals
E₁, E₂the EMFs induced purely by the mutual flux Φₘ inside the ideal core — these are what the turns-ratio relationship a = N₁/N₂ actually applies to
I₁(R₁+jX₁), I₂(R₂+jX₂)the voltage "lost" crossing each winding's own resistance+leakage reactance before/after the ideal core

Crucially, the turns-ratio relationships you learned last week still hold — just for E, not V:

E₁/E₂ = I₂/I₁ = N₁/N₂ = a
Lec 8 · p.13

So the ideal-transformer "core" at the centre of the diagram (Fig. 13) is untouched — it still perfectly relates E₁ to E₂ and the currents through it. All the extra complexity (R₁, X₁, R₂, X₂) just sits outside it, in series, on each side.

The core isn't ideal either: no-load excitation current

Third non-ideality, and this one's about the core material itself, not the windings. A real (non-ideal) core needs some current just to set up the mutual flux Φₘ in the first place (unlike an idealised infinite-permeability core, which would need none), and it also loses real energy to hysteresis and eddy currents as that flux constantly reverses 50/60 times a second. So even with absolutely nothing connected to the secondary (no load at all), the primary winding still draws a small current, called the excitation current or no-load current, I_Φ. Lec 8 · p.14

Analogy This is exactly like a car engine left idling in neutral, not going anywhere (no "load" — no wheels being driven). It still burns fuel just to keep itself running (friction, and just maintaining the spinning parts) — that's the core-loss part. And it still needs to keep its internal moving parts magnetised/aligned in a sense — that's the magnetising part. A transformer draws a small current for the exact same "just staying switched on" reasons, even before you connect any load to the secondary.

Modelling the core: core-loss resistance R_c1 and magnetising reactance jX_m1

The excitation current I_Φ actually has two separate physical jobs, so it's split into two components:

I_Φ = I_c + I_m = E₁/R_c1 + E₁/(jX_m1)
I_c (core-loss current)the part of I_Φ that's in phase with E₁ — it does real work (delivers real power) which is entirely burnt off as hysteresis + eddy-current heat in the core. Modelled as an ordinary resistor R_c1 connected across E₁.
I_m (magnetising current)the part of I_Φ that lags E₁ by exactly 90° — it doesn't dissipate any real power, its only job is to set up the mutual flux Φₘ. Modelled as a reactance jX_m1 connected across E₁, since a current lagging voltage by 90° is exactly what a pure inductive reactance does.

These two branches sit in parallel with each other (both connected across the same E₁), and this parallel combination is called the shunt branch or magnetising branch. Lec 8 · p.15

Core losses: where the R_c1 number actually comes from

The core loss P_c is made of two physical mechanisms — hysteresis loss (energy lost re-aligning magnetic domains every cycle) and eddy-current loss (energy lost to circulating currents induced within the core material itself):

P_h = K_h·B_m^n·f,  (n ≈ 1.5–2.5, take n ≈ 2)       P_e = K_e·B_m²·f²
B_mpeak (maximum) flux density in the core — the single biggest driver of both loss mechanisms
fsupply frequency — fixed for a given transformer (50 Hz or 60 Hz), so it can be treated as a constant here
K_h, K_ematerial/design constants (depend on the core's steel, lamination thickness, geometry) — you never need to know their actual values, they just get absorbed into R_c1

Since f is fixed for a given transformer, both losses reduce to being proportional only to B_m²: P_c = P_h + P_e ∝ B_m². And since the EMF equation from Week 5 says E₁ = 4.44N₁AB_mf (so at fixed f, E₁ ∝ B_m), it follows that P_c ∝ E₁² — the core loss scales with the square of the induced voltage, exactly the way power dissipated in an ordinary resistor scales with the square of the voltage across it (P=V²/R). That's precisely why it's valid to model the whole core-loss mechanism as one resistor R_c1 sitting across E₁: Lec 8 · p.16

P_c = E₁²/R_c1 = I_c²·R_c1
Lec 8 · p.17

The full ("exact") equivalent circuit

Put every non-ideality together — R₁,jX₁ in series with the primary, the shunt branch (R_c1 ∥ jX_m1) hanging across E₁, an (still-perfect) ideal-transformer core, then R₂,jX₂ in series with the secondary, feeding the load Z_L — and you get the full, "exact" equivalent circuit of a real transformer (Fig. 15):

V₁ → R₁,jX₁ → [node E₁: shunt branch R_c1∥jX_m1 hangs down] → ideal core (N₁:N₂) → R₂,jX₂ → V₂ → Z_L
Lec 8 · p.18

This is "exact" in the sense that nothing has been approximated away yet — but notice it's awkward to solve directly from V₁: you can't find I_Φ (which needs E₁) until you already know the current flowing through R₁,jX₁, but that current itself depends on I_Φ. In practice you almost always solve it from the secondary side outward (start from the known load condition on V₂/I₂, work back to E₂, then E₁, then the shunt currents, then I₁, then finally V₁) — which is exactly the order used in the worked example below.

  • Worked example (Lecture 8, Example 2): a 23 kVA, 2300/230 V, 60 Hz transformer has R₁=4Ω, X₁=12Ω, R₂=0.04Ω, X₂=0.12Ω, R_c1=20 kΩ, X_m1=15 kΩ. It delivers 75% of rated load at 0.866 pf (lag) at rated voltage. Find I₁ and V₁. Lec 8 · p.19
  • Take V₂ = 230∠0° V as reference (rated). Load S₂ = 0.75×23 kVA = 17.25 kVA, and cos⁻¹(0.866) = 30°, so S₂ = 17.25∠30° kVA. Load current I₂ = (S₂/V₂)* = 75∠−30° A.
  • Work back through the secondary series impedance: E₂ = V₂ + I₂(R₂+jX₂) = 230∠0° + 75∠−30°×(0.04+j0.12) = 237.18∠1.52° V. Turns ratio a=2300/230=10, so E₁=aE₂=2371.8∠1.52° V, and the ideal-core primary current I_p=I₂/a=7.5∠−30° A. Lec 8 · p.21
  • Shunt branch currents at E₁: I_c=E₁/R_c1=0.1186∠1.52° A, I_m=E₁/(jX_m1)=0.1581∠−88.48° A. Total primary current I₁=I_p+I_c+I_m ≈ 7.683∠−30.55° A. Lec 8 · p.22
  • Finally, V₁=E₁+I₁(R₁+jX₁)=2371.8∠1.52°+7.683∠−30.55°×(4+j12) ≈ 2447.6∠2.97° V, I₁≈7.683 A. Copper loss I₁²R₁+I₂²R₂=461.1 W, core loss E₁²/R_c1=281.3 W. Lec 8 · p.23–24
  • I independently re-ran every complex-number multiplication above by hand — all values check out to the precision shown.

    • Referring impedances between sides: the a² rule

    Notice the ideal-core symbol is still sitting right in the middle of that circuit, which is annoying — you can't just add up a series impedance across it. The fix, exactly as with an ideal transformer, is to "refer" one side's impedance through the turns ratio so the ideal core can be erased entirely and everything redrawn on one single side:

    Referring an impedance from side 2 → side 1 (e.g. secondary → primary):   Z₁,eq = a²·Z₂
    Referring an impedance from side 1 → side 2 (e.g. primary → secondary):   Z₂,eq = Z₁/a²
    a = N₁/N₂turns ratio, primary turns over secondary turns — same a as used for E₁/E₂ and I₂/I₁ last week
    why a², not avoltage transforms by a (V∝N), and current transforms by 1/a (I∝1/N, for constant power) — impedance is V/I, so it transforms by a×a = a². Referring TOWARD the side with MORE turns multiplies by a²; referring TOWARD the side with FEWER turns divides by a².

    Apply this to R₂, X₂ (referring them onto the primary side): the circuit becomes one single loop, no ideal-core symbol left — R₁,jX₁ in series with a²R₂, ja²X₂, feeding a²Z_L. Do the reverse (divide R₁,X₁ by a²) and you get the whole thing referred to the secondary side instead. The shunt branch elements R_c1, X_m1 also carry a "1" subscript specifically because they're already expressed as seen from side 1 (the primary) in this lecture's own convention — referring them to side 2 means dividing by a² too (giving R_c2=R_c1/a², X_m2=X_m1/a²).

    From "exact" to "approximate": moving the shunt branch to the input

    Even after referring everything to one side, the shunt branch still physically sits in the middle of the circuit (at the E₁ node, between R₁+jX₁ and the referred a²R₂+ja²X₂) — this is the same Fig. 15 layout as above. Lec 8 · p.18 That's still awkward: to find I_Φ you need E₁ first, which needs the primary-side current, which itself includes I_Φ — you end up solving it "backwards" from the load, as in the worked example above.

    The standard next simplification — and the one Tutorial 4 explicitly asks you to draw — notices that I_Φ is always tiny (a few tenths of an amp) next to the full-load current (tens of amps). So the voltage it drops across R₁+jX₁ is negligible. This means we can shift the ENTIRE shunt branch from the E₁ node all the way to the input terminals V₁ itself, without materially changing any answer. Once moved, R₁+jX₁ and the referred a²R₂+ja²X₂ are no longer separated by anything — they collapse into ONE single series impedance, with the shunt branch as a separate parallel branch tapped straight across the input. That's the approximate equivalent circuit:

    Referred to HV/primary: V₁ → [shunt R_c1∥jX_m1 straight across input] → series (R₁+a²R₂) + j(X₁+a²X₂) → a²Z_L
    Referred to LV/secondary: → [shunt R_c2∥jX_m2 straight across input] → series (R₁/a²+R₂) + j(X₁/a²+X₂) → Z_L

    Note: this exact "move the shunt branch to the input" step doesn't get its own separate numbered slide in this lecture deck — Lecture 8 stops at the exact circuit (Fig. 15) above. It's the direct, standard next step from the same textbook the lecture itself cites (Guru & Hiziroglu, referenced on the lecture's own References page), and it's exactly what Tutorial 4's title ("...Approximate Equivalent Circuits") and its Q2/Q3 are asking you to draw and use — worked fully below. Lec 8 · p.26 (refs)

    Analogy Imagine an extension cord plugged into a wall socket, with a toaster plugged into the far end of the cord, and a tiny nightlight plugged into a second socket built into the middle of the cord. Solving "exactly" means figuring out the exact voltage at that middle socket first, then working out how much the nightlight actually draws there. The "approximate" shortcut says: the nightlight barely draws any current at all, so just pretend it's plugged in at the wall directly — now you can treat the cord + toaster as one simple series path, and separately just check the nightlight against the (barely changed) wall voltage. You lose a tiny bit of accuracy, but the calculation becomes one straightforward pass instead of a circular one.

    Q2
    Tests: referring impedances with a², and building the approximate equivalent circuit on both the HV and LV sides

    A 200 kVA, 22000/2200 V, 50 Hz single-phase transformer has R₁=5.0Ω, X₁=30Ω, R₂=0.03Ω, X₂=0.1Ω, R_c1=15 kΩ, X_m1=7.5 kΩ. Draw the approximate equivalent circuits referred to the HV side and to the LV side, with proper numerical values.

    Answer

    No numeric answer key was given for this question in the tutorial sheet — everything below is derived directly from the physics (the a² referring rule), not copied from anywhere.

    Step 1 — identify sides and turns ratio. "22000" is the higher voltage, so side 1 (HV) = primary = 22000 V; side 2 (LV) = secondary = 2200 V (this matches R₁,X₁ being the larger numbers — the HV winding has more turns of thinner wire, hence more resistance).

    a = N₁/N₂ = V₁/V₂ = 22000/2200 = 10      a² = 100

    Step 2 — referred to the HV side: keep R₁, X₁, R_c1, X_m1 exactly as given (they're already on side 1). Refer R₂, X₂ up by a²:

    R₂' = a²R₂ = 100×0.03 = 3.0 Ω      X₂' = a²X₂ = 100×0.1 = 10 Ω
    Combined series: (R₁+R₂') + j(X₁+X₂') = (5+3) + j(30+10) = 8 + j40 Ω
    Shunt (straight across input, unchanged): R_c1 = 15 kΩ, X_m1 = 7.5 kΩ

    Step 3 — referred to the LV side: keep R₂, X₂ as given; refer R₁, X₁, R_c1, X_m1 down by a²:

    R₁' = R₁/a² = 5/100 = 0.05 Ω      X₁' = X₁/a² = 30/100 = 0.3 Ω
    R_c2 = R_c1/a² = 15000/100 = 150 Ω      X_m2 = X_m1/a² = 7500/100 = 75 Ω
    Combined series: (R₁'+R₂) + j(X₁'+X₂) = (0.05+0.03) + j(0.3+0.1) = 0.08 + j0.4 Ω

    Sanity check: the HV-side series impedance should be exactly a²=100 times the LV-side one — 8+j40 vs 0.08+j0.4: ratio is exactly 100 on both real and imaginary parts. Same check on the shunt elements: 15kΩ/150Ω=100, 7.5kΩ/75Ω=100. Both check out exactly, confirming the referring was done correctly.

    Q3
    Tests: rated currents, no-load current & core loss, full-load copper loss, and solving the approximate equivalent circuit from both sides

    Nameplate: single-phase, 100 kVA, 11000/2200 V, 60 Hz. (a) Full-load currents on HV and LV sides. (b)–(c) with R₁=6.0Ω, X₁=30.16Ω, R₂=0.28Ω, X₂=1.21Ω, R_c1=125 kΩ, X_m1=60.32 kΩ: find no-load current & pf, no-load core loss, and full-load copper loss. (d) Input voltage and current at full load, 0.85 pf lag, rated secondary voltage as reference. (e) Repeat (d) using the LV-side approximate circuit.

    Answer

    (a) Rated currents:

    I₁,rated = S/V₁ = 100,000/11,000 = 9.09 A      I₂,rated = S/V₂ = 100,000/2,200 = 45.45 A

    Matches the given answer (9.09 A, 45.45 A) exactly — verified: 100000/11000 = 9.0909…, 100000/2200 = 45.4545….

    (b) Turns ratio and the approximate circuit referred to HV:

    a = 11000/2200 = 5      a² = 25
    R_eq1 = R₁ + a²R₂ = 6 + 25×0.28 = 6 + 7 = 13 Ω
    X_eq1 = X₁ + a²X₂ = 30.16 + 25×1.21 = 30.16 + 30.25 = 60.41 Ω
    Shunt branch (unchanged, side 1): R_c1 = 125 kΩ, X_m1 = 60.32 kΩ

    (c)(i) No-load current & pf. At no-load, I₁≈I_Φ is tiny, so essentially all of V₁(rated)=11000 V appears across the shunt branch (this is exactly the "move the shunt to the input" approximation at work):

    I_c = V₁/R_c1 = 11000/125000 = 0.0880 A  (in phase)
    I_m = V₁/X_m1 = 11000/60320 = 0.1824 A  (lagging 90°)
    I_Φ = √(0.0880² + 0.1824²) = 0.202 A ≈ 0.2 A    pf = I_c/I_Φ = 0.0880/0.202 ≈ 0.43–0.44 (lag)

    Matches the given |I_Φ|=0.2 A exactly. The pf works out to ≈0.435 by my own recompute (rounds to 0.43 or 0.44 depending on where you round) — essentially identical to the key's 0.44 (lag); the last digit is just rounding noise from carrying 3 vs 4 sig figs through the square root.

    (c)(ii) No-load (core) loss. Using the same R_c1 model as in the Slides section:

    P_c = V₁²/R_c1 = 11000²/125,000 = 121,000,000/125,000 = 968 W

    Matches the given answer exactly.

    (c)(iii) Full-load copper loss. At full load, the current through the combined series branch equals the rated primary current, I₁,rated = 9.0909 A (from part (a)):

    P_cu = I₁,rated²×R_eq1 = 9.0909² × 13 = 82.6446 × 13 = 1074.4 W ≈ 1074 W

    Matches the given answer exactly.

    (d) Input voltage & current at full load, 0.85 pf lag, HV-side approximate circuit. Take V₂ = 2200∠0° V as reference.

  • I₂ = (S₂/V₂)* with S₂=100kVA∠cos⁻¹(0.85) = 100,000∠31.79° VA (lag ⇒ current lags): I₂ = 45.4545∠−31.79° A = 38.636 − j23.945 A.
  • Refer to HV side: I₂' = I₂/a = 9.0909∠−31.79° A = 7.727 − j4.789 A (this is the current through the combined series branch).
  • Refer V₂ to HV: V₂' = a·V₂ = 5×2200 = 11000∠0° V. Solve for input voltage: V₁ = V₂' + I₂'(R_eq1+jX_eq1) = 11000 + (7.727−j4.789)(13+j60.41) = 11000 + (389.8+j404.6) = 11389.8+j404.6 V, so |V₁| ≈ 11397 V (matches the given 11397 V exactly), at ∠2.04°.
  • Now the shunt branch sits right at this V₁ (approximate model): I_c=V₁/R_c1=0.0918+j0.0032 A, I_m=V₁/(jX_m1)=0.0067−j0.1888 A, so I_Φ=0.0978−j0.1856 A.
  • Total input current I₁ = I₂' + I_Φ = (7.727+0.098) − j(4.789+0.186) = 7.825 − j4.975 A, so |I₁| ≈ 9.27 A.

    • V₁ matches the given 11397 V exactly. My own recompute gives I₁≈9.27 A against the key's 9.26 A — a ~0.1% gap, well inside normal rounding accumulated over several complex-number steps (the same kind of small drift the Week 3 page flagged for a different question). I re-verified this same 9.27 A independently via the LV-side circuit in part (e) below, so I'm confident 9.27 A (not 9.26 A) is the more precise value — but they agree to 3 significant figures either way.

    (e) Repeat (d) using the LV-side approximate circuit — should land on the same answer.

  • Refer R₁,X₁,R_c1,X_m1 down to the LV side: R_eq2=R₁/a²+R₂=6/25+0.28=0.24+0.28=0.52 Ω; X_eq2=X₁/a²+X₂=30.16/25+1.21=1.206+1.21=2.416 Ω; R_c2=125000/25=5000 Ω; X_m2=60320/25=2412.8 Ω.
  • Sanity check against part (b): R_eq2×a²=0.52×25=13=R_eq1 ✓, X_eq2×a²=2.416×25=60.41=X_eq1 ✓ — the referring is self-consistent.
  • Now stay on the natural (unreferred) secondary side: V₂=2200∠0° V, I₂=45.4545∠−31.79° A=38.636−j23.945 A (same as in (d), no referring needed this time since we're already on side 2).
  • Find the (referred) input node: V₁,LV = V₂ + I₂(R_eq2+jX_eq2) = 2200 + (38.636−j23.945)(0.52+j2.416) = 2200+(78.0+j80.9) = 2278.0+j80.9 V → |V₁,LV| ≈ 2279.4 V.
  • Check: V₁,LV×a = 2279.4×5 = 11397.0 V — matches part (d)'s V₁ exactly. Good.
  • Shunt branch at V₁,LV: I_c2=V₁,LV/R_c2=0.4556+j0.0162 A, I_m2=V₁,LV/(jX_m2)=0.0335−j0.9441 A, so I_Φ2 (referred to LV) = 0.4891−j0.9279 A.
  • Total current at the referred input node (this equals a×I₁, the actual primary current referred UP to the LV side): I₂+I_Φ2 = 39.13−j24.87 A. Divide by a=5 to get the actual primary current: I₁ = 7.825−j4.975 A → |I₁| ≈ 9.27 A — identical (to 4 sig figs) to the value found in part (d).

    • Both circuits — HV-referred and LV-referred — agree with each other (V₁=11397 V both ways, I₁≈9.27 A both ways), which is exactly the consistency check this question is really testing: it shouldn't matter which side you choose to do the algebra on, as long as the referring is done correctly.

    Copy these into your own notes:

    • Real transformer non-idealities, in order: winding resistance R₁,R₂ (series, from copper) → leakage reactance X₁,X₂ (series, from flux that misses the other winding) → core-loss resistance R_c1 (shunt, models hysteresis+eddy heat, P_c=E₁²/R_c1) → magnetising reactance X_m1 (shunt, models the current needed to set up Φₘ, lags E₁ by 90°).
    • Series relations: V₁=E₁+I₁(R₁+jX₁), E₂=V₂+I₂(R₂+jX₂). Turns-ratio relations still apply to E (not V): E₁/E₂=I₂/I₁=N₁/N₂=a.
    • No-load current I_Φ=I_c+I_m=E₁/R_c1 + E₁/(jX_m1); at no-load the primary draws I_Φ only, and E₁≈V₁ (rated) since I_Φ is small.
    • Referring impedance between sides: Z toward the side with MORE turns ×a²; Z toward the side with FEWER turns ÷a². (a=N₁/N₂.)
    • Exact equivalent circuit: shunt branch sits at the E₁ node, between the primary and secondary series impedances — must be solved from the load side backward.
    • Approximate equivalent circuit: shunt branch moved to sit directly across the input terminals (valid since I_Φ≪I_rated) — collapses the two series impedances into ONE combined series branch, solvable in a single forward pass. This is the circuit Tutorial-style "draw the approximate equivalent circuit" questions want.
    • Core loss P_c ∝ B_m² ∝ E₁² at constant frequency — same reasoning as P=V²/R for an ordinary resistor, which is exactly why R_c1 is a valid model.
