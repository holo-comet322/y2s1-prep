Why a single number (a "point estimate") isn't enough

Every week so far, you've been calculating a single sample mean x̄ or sample variance s² and treating it as your best guess at the unknown population value (μ or σ²). But a single number gives no sense of how confident you should be — was your sample lucky and close to μ, or unlucky and far off? A confidence interval fixes this by giving a RANGE of plausible values, together with a stated confidence level (like 95%) attached to that range.

Analogy Think of a point estimate as a single dart throw at a target — it tells you nothing about your aim. A confidence interval is more like saying "I'm 95% confident the bullseye is somewhere inside this circle I've drawn around my throw." The circle's size reflects how much uncertainty is baked into your one throw — a wobblier throw (smaller sample, bigger spread) needs a bigger circle to still be 95% confident it contains the target.

Critically, once a specific numeric interval like [4.02, 4.38] has been computed, μ either IS or IS NOT inside it — there's no more randomness left at that point. The 95% describes the PROCEDURE (if you repeated this sampling process many times, 95% of the resulting intervals would contain μ), not this one specific interval. The correct phrasing is always "we are 95% confident that μ lies in [4.02, 4.38]," never "there's a 95% probability μ is in this interval."

Confidence interval for μ, when σ is known

All three confidence-interval formulas this week share the exact same shape: [point estimate − margin of error, point estimate + margin of error]. What changes each time is only how the margin of error E is calculated.

[x̄ − E, x̄ + E],  where E = z_{α/2} × (σ/√n)
x̄your sample mean — the center of the interval γ (confidence level)e.g. 95% — how confident you want to be; define α = 1−γ (e.g. γ=0.95 → α=0.05) z_{α/2}the z-value cutting off α/2 in EACH tail of the standard normal curve, found from Table A1 — e.g. for 95% confidence, z_{0.025}=1.96 σ/√nthe standard error of the sample mean — this is exactly the σ_X̄ formula from Week 5, reused here directly

This formula is really just last week's "sample mean is approximately N(μ, σ²/n)" fact, rearranged. Instead of asking "what's the probability X̄ falls within z_{α/2} standard errors of μ?" (a Week 5 style question), we now flip the question around: "given the X̄ we actually observed, what range of μ values would have made this X̄ plausible?"

Worked example: a survey scores agreement 1–5 on "it's cool to be an entrepreneur," σ=0.9, sample of n=100 gives x̄=4.2. Construct a 95% CI for μ.

  • Confirm CLT applies: n=100≥30, so X̄ is approximately normal even without knowing the population shape.
  • Find z_{α/2}: γ=0.95 → α=0.05 → α/2=0.025 → from Table A1, z_{0.025}=1.96.
  • Compute the margin of error: E = 1.96 × (0.9/√100) = 1.96 × 0.09 = 0.1764.
  • Build the interval: [4.2−0.1764, 4.2+0.1764] = [4.024, 4.376].

    • Interpretation: "we are 95% confident the true average opinion score across the whole population lies between about 4.02 and 4.38" — since this range sits above 4 (Agree), the population leans toward agreeing that entrepreneurship is 'cool.'

    Confidence interval for μ, when σ is unknown: the t-distribution

    In real life you almost never actually know σ — you only have your sample's own standard deviation, s. Simply plugging s in for σ and reusing the z formula introduces extra uncertainty (since s itself is just an estimate, noisier for small samples), so a different, slightly WIDER-tailed distribution is used instead: the t-distribution.

    t = (X̄ − μ)/(S/√n)  follows a t-distribution with (n−1) degrees of freedom    [x̄ − E, x̄ + E],  E = t_{α/2} × (s/√n)
    S (or s)the SAMPLE standard deviation, computed from your data, used in place of the unknown σ t_{α/2}, (n−1) d.f.read off a DIFFERENT table (Table A3), using degrees of freedom = n−1 — NOT the same table as z
    Analogy The t-distribution looks like the normal curve but with fatter, heavier tails — think of it as the normal curve's more "cautious" cousin. Because you're using an ESTIMATED s instead of the true σ, there's an extra layer of uncertainty on top of the usual sampling variability, and the fatter tails build in some extra room for that added uncertainty. As the sample size n grows, s becomes a more and more reliable stand-in for σ, and the t-distribution gradually converges to look just like the standard normal (this is why the shape depends on degrees of freedom n−1 — more data, less extra caution needed).

    Rule of thumb worth memorizing: for n≥30 (large samples), t_{α/2} is close enough to z_{α/2} that some textbooks just use z directly as an approximation, since Table A3 typically doesn't even list degrees of freedom that high.

    Worked example: six sliming-process participants lost weight (kg): 0.46, 0.61, 0.52, 0.48, 0.57, 0.54. Find a 95% CI for the mean weight loss.

  • Since n=6 is small, we must ASSUME X is (approximately) normally distributed to justify using the t-distribution at all — this assumption should always be stated explicitly for small samples.
  • Compute the sample statistics: x̄=0.53, s=0.0559.
  • Find t_{α/2} with df=n−1=5: for 95% confidence, t_{0.025}=2.571 (from Table A3 — notice this is bigger than the z equivalent of 1.96, reflecting the extra uncertainty from a small sample and unknown σ).
  • Margin of error: E = 2.571 × (0.0559/√6) ≈ 0.059.
  • Interval: [0.53−0.059, 0.53+0.059] = [0.471, 0.589] kg.

    • Confidence interval for σ²: the chi-square distribution

    Sometimes what you actually care about estimating isn't the average, but the VARIABILITY itself — e.g. a pharmaceutical company needs a drug's potency to be consistent (low variance), not just correctly averaged. This reuses last week's chi-square result directly.

    (n−1)S²/σ² ∼ chi-square with (n−1) d.f.   ⇒   γ CI for σ²:  [ (n−1)s²/χ²_{α/2},  (n−1)s²/χ²_{1−α/2} ]
    s²the sample variance you computed from your data χ²_{α/2}the chi-square value with area α/2 to its RIGHT — this is the LARGER critical value, and (importantly) it goes in the DENOMINATOR of the LOWER bound χ²_{1−α/2}the chi-square value with area (1−α/2) to its right (equivalently, α/2 to its LEFT) — the SMALLER critical value, goes in the denominator of the UPPER bound

    This is the one formula this week that DOESN'T follow the "estimate ± E" shape — because the chi-square distribution is skewed (not symmetric like z or t), the interval is built from two genuinely different critical values rather than one margin added and subtracted symmetrically. The easiest way to avoid mixing up which critical value goes where: the BIGGER critical value (χ²_{α/2}) divides into the LOWER bound (making it smaller), and the SMALLER critical value (χ²_{1−α/2}) divides into the UPPER bound (making it bigger) — dividing by a bigger number always shrinks the result.

    Worked example: potency measurements from 20 painkillers give sample variance s²=0.0018. Find a 98% CI for σ².

  • df = n−1 = 19. γ=0.98 → α=0.02 → α/2=0.01.
  • Look up both critical values from the chi-square table with df=19: χ²_{0.01}=36.19 (bigger, goes in lower bound's denominator), χ²_{0.99}=7.63 (smaller, goes in upper bound's denominator).
  • Lower bound: (19)(0.0018)/36.19 ≈ 0.000945. Upper bound: (19)(0.0018)/7.63 ≈ 0.00448.
  • 98% CI for σ²: [0.000945, 0.00448]. (Taking square roots of both ends gives the corresponding CI for σ itself, if needed.)

    Q1
    Tests: confidence interval for μ, σ known — the most straightforward of the three formulas

    A machine drills holes with known σ=0.04mm (unchanged after resetting). 100 holes sampled have mean diameter 5.2mm. Give a 95% confidence interval for the mean diameter.

    Answer

    Step 1 — identify which formula applies: σ is GIVEN (0.04mm), so use the z-based formula, not t. σ=0.04, n=100, x̄=5.2, γ=0.95.

    Step 2 — check CLT applies (n≥30) so X̄ can be treated as approximately normal, then find z_{α/2}. α=0.05, α/2=0.025, so z_{0.025}=1.96.

    Step 3 — compute the margin of error and build the interval.

    E = 1.96 × (0.04/√100) = 1.96 × 0.004 = 0.00784
    CI = [5.2 − 0.00784, 5.2 + 0.00784] = [5.19216, 5.20784]

    I verified this using scipy.stats.norm.ppf — matches the answer key exactly. Notice how NARROW this interval is (only ±0.008mm) — that's the payoff of a large sample (n=100) combined with a small σ: lots of data plus low natural variability gives a very precise estimate.

    Q2
    Tests: computing sample mean/sd from raw data, then the t-based confidence interval for μ when σ is unknown

    14 dentists' depth-perception estimation errors (mm): 1.1, 1.5, 0.9, 2.1, 1.4, 1.7, 0.8, 1.3, 1.8, 1.1, 1.6, 1.9, 1.2, 1.6. (a) Find the sample mean and sd. (b) Find a 90% CI for the population mean error.

    Answer

    Part (a) — compute directly from the definitions (Week 5's x̄ = (1/n)Σxᵢ and s² = (1/(n−1))Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²).

    x̄ = (1/14)Σxᵢ ≈ 1.4286 mm    s ≈ 0.3832 mm

    Part (b) Step 1 — σ is NOT given anywhere in the question, only the sample's own numbers, so this must use the t-distribution. n=14 (small, under 30), so we must EXPLICITLY assume the errors are approximately normally distributed to justify using the t-interval — state this assumption.

    Step 2 — find t_{α/2} with df=n−1=13. γ=0.90 → α=0.10 → α/2=0.05 → t_{0.05} with 13 d.f. ≈ 1.771 (from Table A3).

    Step 3 — compute the margin of error and build the interval.

    E = 1.771 × (0.3832/√14) ≈ 0.1814
    CI = [1.4286 − 0.1814, 1.4286 + 0.1814] ≈ [1.249, 1.611]

    I recomputed the raw sample mean/sd and the full t-interval numerically in Python (scipy.stats.t.ppf) — both parts match the answer key exactly.

    Q3
    Tests: confidence interval for σ² using the chi-square distribution, including correctly matching each critical value to its bound

    20 students' GPAs (assumed normally distributed): 2.28, 2.62, 3.04, 2.81, 2.82, 2.50, 2.86, 2.88, 3.22, 2.68, 3.00, 2.50, 3.28, 2.79, 2.64, 2.49, 3.15, 2.84, 3.00, 2.63. Determine the 95% confidence interval for the population variance of GPAs.

    Answer

    Step 1 — this asks for VARIANCE specifically, so use the chi-square formula, not z or t. First compute the sample variance from the data: s² ≈ 0.0698 (using n−1=19 in the denominator, as always for sample variance).

    Step 2 — find both chi-square critical values with df=n−1=19. γ=0.95 → α=0.05 → α/2=0.025. From the chi-square table: χ²_{0.025} ≈ 32.85 (bigger, for the lower bound), χ²_{0.975} ≈ 8.91 (smaller, for the upper bound).

    Step 3 — plug into the two-sided (asymmetric) formula.

    Lower bound = (19)(0.0698)/32.85 ≈ 0.0404
    Upper bound = (19)(0.0698)/8.91 ≈ 0.149
    95% CI for σ² = [0.04, 0.149]

    I verified this fully numerically (computing s² directly from the 20 raw values, then both chi-square critical values via scipy.stats.chi2.ppf) — matches the answer key exactly.

    Worth noticing: unlike Q1 and Q2, this interval is NOT centered on s²=0.0698 — the lower bound (0.0404) is only about 0.03 below s², but the upper bound (0.149) is about 0.08 above it. That lopsidedness is the direct fingerprint of the chi-square distribution's skew, and is exactly why this formula can't be written as a simple "estimate ± E."

    ✍️ Write This Down

    Copy these into your own notes:

    • Every CI this week has the shape [estimate − E, estimate + E], EXCEPT the variance interval (chi-square is skewed, so it uses two different critical values instead).
    • σ known → z-interval: E = z_{α/2}(σ/√n), read z_{α/2} from Table A1.
    • σ unknown → t-interval: E = t_{α/2}(s/√n), read t_{α/2} from Table A3 using df=n−1. For n≥30, t_{α/2}≈z_{α/2} as an approximation.
    • Variance CI (chi-square, df=n−1): [(n−1)s²/χ²_{α/2}, (n−1)s²/χ²_{1−α/2}] — bigger critical value in the lower bound's denominator, smaller critical value in the upper bound's.
    • Always state explicitly whether X being normal is GIVEN, ASSUMED (needed for small n<30), or guaranteed by the CLT (n≥30) — this justification is part of a complete answer, not just the final numbers.
    • Correct interpretation: "we are γ% confident μ lies in this interval" — never "there's a γ% probability μ is in this interval" (μ is fixed; the interval is what varies from sample to sample).

    👁️ Visualize It

    The clearest way to internalize "confidence level" is to imagine repeating the whole sampling process many times, each time drawing a NEW interval — most land on the true μ, but a small fraction (on average, exactly 1−γ of them) miss it entirely.

    Each horizontal bar is one possible [x̄−E, x̄+E] interval from a different random sample — all built the exact same way, just from different data. At a 90% confidence level, on average 9 out of every 10 such intervals contain the true μ (dashed line) — the 1-in-10 miss shown here in a different color isn't a mistake, it's the built-in, expected rate of misses that "90% confident" always allows for.

    Why this picture matters for interpreting YOUR interval

    • Once you compute one specific interval like [5.19216, 5.20784], you cannot tell from the number alone whether it's a "hit" or a "miss" bar in this picture — that's the whole reason the correct language is "95% confident," describing the reliability of the METHOD across many repeats, not a probability statement about this one already-computed interval.
