📡 Exam radar — Week 9
  • Laplace is worth 15–30 marks in every final (25 or more in 5 of 6 papers, usually Question 3). The transform half is almost always the same: “find L{f(t)}” where f has a pulse or a delayed/exponentially-damped piece (2023-24 S1 & S2, 2024-25 S1 & S2). The single most-tested skill: rewrite the function in shifted form g(t−a)u(t−a) before using e−asG(s) (concept 5).
  • Typical traps seen in the finals: t·u(t−a) must become (t−a)u(t−a) + a·u(t−a); an e−3t sitting next to u(t−1) must be split as e−3·e−3(t−1); and sin(t−1)/cos(t−1) are already in shifted form — do not expand them.

Concept 1 of 6

Step function u(t) and delta function δ(t)

u(t) = 0 for t < 0,   1 for t ≥ 0   |   δ(t) = du/dt   |   ∫−∞∞ δ(t) dt = 1
u(t)the unit step: a switch that turns on at t = 0 and stays on. u(t − a) turns on at t = a instead (shifted right by a).
δ(t)the delta (impulse): infinitely tall, infinitely thin spike at t = 0 with total area exactly 1. It is the derivative of the step (the step jumps by 1 in zero time).
sifting∫ f(t)δ(t − t0) dt = f(t0): multiplying by a delta and integrating picks out the value of f at the spike.
Analogyu(t) is a light switch flipped on at time 0. δ(t) is the flick of the switch itself — an instantaneous hammer-blow whose total “amount” is 1 (a drum-stick strike, a sudden voltage spike).

Building pulses (used in nearly every final): a rectangular pulse of height 1 from t = 0 to t = 1 is u(t) − u(t−1): switch on at 0, switch off at 1 (a second, negative switch). Likewise any piecewise function can be written as a sum of shifted steps — the height changes at each jump become the coefficients.

Check your understanding — write g(t) = 1 on [0,2], 2 on [2,3], 0 elsewhere using steps (Tutorial 10 Q2)

Start with u(t) (height 1 from t = 0). At t = 2 the height rises by 1 ⇒ add u(t−2). At t = 3 the height drops from 2 to 0 ⇒ subtract 2u(t−3). g(t) = u(t) + u(t−2) − 2u(t−3). Check at t = 2.5: 1 + 1 − 0 = 2 ✓; at t = 4: 1 + 1 − 2 = 0 ✓.

Concept 2 of 6

Convolution: x(t) ∗ h(t)

x(t) ∗ h(t) = ∫−∞∞ x(τ) h(t − τ) dτ

Recipe: (1) flip h (because of −τ); (2) slide it by t; (3) multiply by x(τ); (4) integrate the overlap. Two special facts: x ∗ δ = x (δ is the identity for convolution) and (Week 11) L{x ∗ h} = X(s)·H(s).

AnalogyA pond (the system h) being hit by many pebbles (the input x) at different times. The ripple pattern at time t is the sum of each pebble's ripple, scaled by pebble size and delayed by when it hit — convolution is that sum.
Lecture example — y = e−tu(t) ∗ u(t), find y(1)

y(1) = ∫ e−τu(τ)u(1−τ) dτ. The product u(τ)u(1−τ) is 1 only for 0 ≤ τ ≤ 1, so y(1) = ∫01 e−τ dτ = 1 − e−1 = 0.632.

Concept 3 of 6

Definition of the Laplace transform

F(s) = L{f(t)} = ∫0∞ f(t) e−st dt
f(t)a signal in the time domain. This course is “single-sided”: f(t) = 0 for t < 0 and integration starts at t = 0.
sa complex number s = σ + jω. The factor e−st acts as a “probe” that measures how much of each growth/oscillation rate is present in f.
F(s)the same signal in the s-domain. Calculus (derivatives, integrals) becomes algebra (multiply/divide by s) — that is the whole point.
AnalogyTaking the Laplace transform is like translating a recipe into a language where hard steps (“simmer then reduce”) turn into simple multiplication. You solve the problem in the easy language, then translate back (inverse transform, Week 10).
Do it from the definition — the three basic transforms (lecture)
  1. u(t): ∫0∞ e−st dt = [−e−st/s]0∞ = 0 − (−1/s) = 1/s.
  2. e−atu(t): ∫ e−(s+a)t dt = 1/(s+a).
  3. δ(t): ∫ δ(t)e−st dt = e0 = 1 (sifting).
  4. u(t−2): ∫2∞ e−st dt = e−2s/s. (Starting the integral at 2 is what produces the e−2s.)

⚠ The #1 skill: making the delay visible

The delay rule only works if every t in the function is written as (t − t0) — the function and the step must be delayed by the same amount. If it isn't, force it:

  • t·u(t−a) → (t−a)u(t−a) + a·u(t−a)   (because t = (t−a) + a)
  • t²u(t−1) → [(t−1)² + 2(t−1) + 1]u(t−1)
  • e−3tu(t−1) → e−3·e−3(t−1)u(t−1)
  • sin(t−1)u(t−1) is already shifted — do not expand it; just use e−s·1/(s²+1).
Lecture worked example 1 — L{sin t cos 2t u(t)} (no product rule exists: turn the product into a sum)
  1. sin a cos b = ½[sin(a+b) + sin(a−b)] ⇒ sin t cos 2t = ½[sin 3t + sin(−t)] = ½(sin 3t − sin t).
  2. L = ½[3/(s²+9) − 1/(s²+1)].
Lecture worked example 2 — L{(t² − 2t + 1)e−5tu(t−1)}
  1. t² − 2t + 1 = (t−1)². Write e−5t = e−5e−5(t−1), so f(t) = e−5·g(t−1)u(t−1) with g(τ) = τ²e−5τ.
  2. G(s) = 2/(s+5)³ (table, with the s-shift). Delay by 1: F(s) = 2e−5e−s/(s+5)³.
Lecture worked example 3 — L{t cos t u(t)} using the “multiply by t” rule
  1. L{cos t} = s/(s²+1).
  2. G(s) = −d/ds[s/(s²+1)] = −[(s²+1) − 2s²]/(s²+1)² = (s² − 1)/(s²+1)².
Concept 6 of 6

Stable vs unstable responses (read it from the poles)

A response y(t)u(t) is stable if it stays bounded for all t ≥ 0, unstable if it grows without limit. To judge from Y(s) = N(s)/D(s) (after cancelling common factors): find the poles (roots of D(s) = 0).

PolesTime behaviourVerdict
all Re(s) < 0 (left half-plane)decays: e−t, e−tsin tstable
on the imaginary axis, simple (not repeated)sustained oscillation: sin t, u(t) (pole at 0)stable (bounded)
any pole with Re(s) > 0grows: et, etsin tunstable
a repeated pole on the imaginary axist·(…) grows linearly: t u(t), t cos t u(t)unstable
AnalogyPoles are where the system “resonates”. A pole in the left half-plane is a swing with friction (motion dies out). On the axis is a frictionless swing (keeps swinging, never worse). In the right half-plane it is a swing that pumps itself higher and higher. A repeated pole on the axis is being pushed at exactly its natural rhythm — resonance, growing without bound.

Lecture examples: t e−tu(t) → 1/(s+1)², double pole at −1 ⇒ stable. t cos t u(t) → (s²−1)/(s²+1)², double poles at ±j ⇒ unstable.

Tutorial 10, Q1
Tests: definition of L

Compute the Laplace transforms from the definition: (a) e−2tu(t); (b) sin 3t u(t); (c) t u(t); (d) (t−2)u(t−2).

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Answer
  1. (a) ∫0∞ e−(s+2)t dt = 1/(s+2).
  2. (b) Write sin 3t = (ej3t − e−j3t)/(2j). Then L = (1/2j)[1/(s−3j) − 1/(s+3j)] = (1/2j)·6j/(s²+9) = 3/(s²+9). (Or integrate by parts twice.)
  3. (c) ∫0∞ t e−st dt: by parts, [−t e−st/s]0∞ + (1/s)∫e−stdt = 0 + 1/s² = 1/s².
  4. (d) Substitute τ = t−2: ∫2∞(t−2)e−stdt = e−2s∫0∞ τe−sτdτ = e−2s/s².

Tutorial 10, Q2
Tests: step functions, delay rule

g(t) = 1 for 0 ≤ t ≤ 2; 2 for 2 ≤ t ≤ 3; 0 elsewhere. (a) Sketch and write g with delayed steps. (b) Find L{g}.

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Answer
  1. (a) g(t) = u(t) + u(t−2) − 2u(t−3) (a staircase: height 1, then 2, then back to 0).
  2. (b) L{u(t−a)} = e−as/s ⇒ G(s) = (1 + e−2s − 2e−3s)/s.
  3. Sanity check: the area under g is 1×2 + 2×1 = 4, and G(0) should equal that area. As s→0 the numerator vanishes, so use the derivative of the numerator: −2 + 6 = 4 ✓.

Tests: shift rules

Using properties and the basic table, find L of: (a) sin(t−2)u(t−2); (b) e−(t−2) sin(t−2)u(t−2); (c) e−t sin(t−2)u(t−2); (d) cos²t u(t).

Try it yourself first, then reveal the answer
Answer
  1. (a) already shifted by 2: e−2s/(s²+1). (The printed key shows “s²+a” — a typo for s²+1.)
  2. (b) g(τ) = e−τsin τ has G = 1/((s+1)²+1); delayed by 2: e−2s/(s²+2s+2).
  3. (c) e−t = e−2e−(t−2), so f = e−2 × (part b): e−2s−2/(s²+2s+2).
  4. (d) cos²t = (1 + cos 2t)/2 ⇒ 1/(2s) + s/(2(s²+4)).

transform of a pulse (t on [0,1])inverse with repeated pole at 0

(i) Find the Laplace transform of f(t) = 0 (t < 0); t (0 ≤ t ≤ 1); 0 (t > 1). (ii) Find the inverse transform of F(s) = 2/[s²(s + 2)].

Try it yourself first, then reveal the solution
Solution
  1. (i) Write the pulse with step functions: f(t) = t[u(t) − u(t−1)] = t u(t) − t u(t−1). The second term must be re-written in shifted form (t−1): t = (t−1) + 1, so t u(t−1) = (t−1)u(t−1) + u(t−1).
  2. f(t) = t u(t) − (t−1)u(t−1) − u(t−1). Use L{tu(t)} = 1/s², L{g(t−a)u(t−a)} = e−asG(s), L{u(t−1)} = e−s/s:
    F(s) = 1/s² − e−s/s² − e−s/s = [1 − e−s(1 + s)]/s²
  3. (ii) Partial fractions: 2/[s²(s+2)] = A/s + B/s² + C/(s+2) ⇒ 2 = As(s+2) + B(s+2) + Cs². s = 0: 2B = 2 ⇒ B = 1. s = −2: 4C = 2 ⇒ C = ½. s² coefficient: A + C = 0 ⇒ A = −½.
  4. Invert: f(t) = [−½ + t + ½e−2t] u(t). (Check: f(0) = −0.5 + 0.5 = 0 ✓.)

Final 2023-24 Sem 2 · Q3(a) · 15 marks

exponential + shift togetherinverse of shifted rational function

(i) Find L{ [t² + 4cos(t−1)] e−3t u(t−1) }. (ii) Find the inverse transform of F(s) = (3s + 3)e−2s/(s² − 4s + 8).

Try it yourself first, then reveal the solution
Solution
  1. (i) Make everything a function of (t−1): e−3t = e−3e−3(t−1), and t² = (t−1)² + 2(t−1) + 1.
  2. So f(t) = e−3 · g(t−1)u(t−1) with g(τ) = e−3τ[τ² + 2τ + 1 + 4cos τ].
  3. G(s): use L{e−ath(t)} = H(s+a) with a = 3: G(s) = 2/(s+3)³ + 2/(s+3)² + 1/(s+3) + 4(s+3)/[(s+3)² + 1].
  4. F(s) = e−3e−s G(s) = e−(s+3)[2/(s+3)³ + 2/(s+3)² + 1/(s+3) + 4(s+3)/((s+3)²+1)].
  5. (ii) Complete the square: s² − 4s + 8 = (s−2)² + 4. Numerator 3s + 3 = 3(s−2) + 9. So (3s+3)/[(s−2)²+4] = 3(s−2)/[(s−2)²+4] + (9/2)·2/[(s−2)²+4].
  6. Inverse (before the delay): e2t[3 cos 2t + 4.5 sin 2t]. The e−2s delays by 2: f(t) = e2(t−2)[3cos 2(t−2) + 4.5 sin 2(t−2)] u(t−2).

✍️ Write This Down

Copy these into your own notes:

  • L{f} = ∫0∞ f(t)e−stdt (single-sided; f = 0 for t < 0). u → 1/s; δ → 1; e−at → 1/(s+a); tn/n! → 1/sn+1; sin ωt → ω/(s²+ω²); cos ωt → s/(s²+ω²).
  • Shift in s: e−atf(t) → F(s+a). Shift in t: f(t−t0)u(t−t0) → e−t0sF(s). Always rewrite so the function and the step carry the same (t−t0).
  • Pulse of height 1 from a to b: u(t−a) − u(t−b). Staircase/piecewise: add a step for each jump (coefficient = jump size).
  • tf(t) → −F′(s). f′ → sF−f(0). f″ → s²F − sf(0) − f′(0). ∫f → F/s. f∗g → FG.
  • No product rule for L: use identities to turn products (sin·cos, cos²) into sums first.
  • Stability: all poles in the left half-plane (or simple on the imaginary axis) ⇒ stable; any right-half-plane pole or repeated imaginary pole ⇒ unstable.

👁️ Visualize It

The s-plane. Each dot is a pole of Y(s). The further left a pole sits, the faster that piece of the response dies; poles on the vertical axis oscillate forever; poles on the right blow up. A repeated pole on the axis (like the double poles at ±j for t cos t) also blows up — resonance.
